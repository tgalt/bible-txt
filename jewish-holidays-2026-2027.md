# The Jewish Holidays, 2026 and 2027

## Every festival, fast, and observance, with its Hebrew date and the evening it begins

---

## How to Read This Document

### Every date here is a Hebrew date wearing a Gregorian costume

The Jewish calendar is lunisolar: months follow the moon, years are kept in step with the sun by adding a thirteenth month seven times in every nineteen. So a holiday sits on a *fixed* Hebrew date — Passover is always 15 Nisan — and that fixed date slides against the Gregorian calendar from year to year. The 2026 and 2027 columns of the tables below hold the same festivals on different dates; only the costume changes.

### The day begins the evening before

This is the single most important thing on this page, and the thing most often got wrong. A Jewish day runs from sunset to sunset, not from midnight — the pattern set in Genesis 1:5, *"And the evening and the morning were the first day."* A holiday listed as Thursday begins at sundown on **Wednesday**.

Every table here therefore gives two columns:

- **Begins at sundown** — the evening the holiday starts. This is the night of the Passover seder, the first Chanukah candle, the Kol Nidrei service.
- **Observed** — the daytime date (or dates) the holiday is named for, the date a calendar will print.

If you need to be somewhere for the start of a holiday, use the **first** column.

Sundown times vary by latitude and longitude, and observance customarily begins a little before sunset and ends at nightfall. For an actual time, check a local candle-lighting time for your city.

### Israel and the Diaspora keep some of these differently

Outside the land of Israel, several festivals are kept for an extra day — *yom tov sheni shel galuyot*, the second festival day of the exile, a survival of the era when the new moon was fixed by witnesses in Jerusalem and word took time to travel. The main tables below give the **Diaspora** reckoning, which is the longer one. [Where Israel differs](#where-israel-differs) lists every difference, with dates.

### Which spellings

Hebrew names are given in the common English transliteration, with alternatives in the notes: *Sukkot* (Succoth, Tabernacles), *Shavuot* (Shavuos, Pentecost), *Pesach* (Passover), *Chanukah* (Hanukkah). Nothing hangs on the choice.

A few things worth noticing before the full lists:

- **Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat in both years** (Sep 12, 2026 and Oct 2, 2027). When the first day of Rosh Hashanah is Shabbat the shofar is not sounded that day; it is sounded only on the second day — Sunday, Sep 13, 2026 and Sunday, Oct 3, 2027.
- **Yom Kippur falls on a Monday in both years**, which means the fast begins Sunday evening.

## Where Israel Differs

In the land of Israel four of the festivals are kept one day shorter than in the Diaspora, and Shemini Atzeret and Simchat Torah are a single day rather than two. Everything else on this page — all the fasts, Purim, Chanukah, Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, the minor days — is identical in both.

| Festival | Diaspora | Israel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pesach 2026** | Thu, Apr 2 – Thu, Apr 9 (8 days) | Thu, Apr 2 – Wed, Apr 8 (7 days) |
| **Pesach 2027** | Thu, Apr 22 – Thu, Apr 29 (8 days) | Thu, Apr 22 – Wed, Apr 28 (7 days) |
| **Shavuot 2026** | Fri, May 22 – Sat, May 23 (2 days) | Fri, May 22 (1 day) |
| **Shavuot 2027** | Fri, Jun 11 – Sat, Jun 12 (2 days) | Fri, Jun 11 (1 day) |
| **Simchat Torah 2026** | Sun, Oct 4, the day after Shemini Atzeret | Sat, Oct 3, combined with Shemini Atzeret |
| **Simchat Torah 2027** | Sun, Oct 24, the day after Shemini Atzeret | Sat, Oct 23, combined with Shemini Atzeret |

The festival days on which work is not done (*yom tov*) also differ:

| | Diaspora | Israel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pesach 2026** | Apr 2–3 and Apr 8–9 | Apr 2 and Apr 8 |
| **Pesach 2027** | Apr 22–23 and Apr 28–29 | Apr 22 and Apr 28 |
| **Sukkot 2026** | Sep 26–27 | Sep 26 |
| **Sukkot 2027** | Oct 16–17 | Oct 16 |

The intervening days of Pesach and Sukkot are *chol hamoed*, the weekdays of the festival: the festival continues, but ordinary work is largely permitted.

## The Seasons Around the Holidays

Several holidays are the endpoints of a period rather than isolated days. These spans are worth having on a calendar in their own right.

| Period | 2026 | 2027 | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Counting of the Omer** (49 days) | Fri, Apr 3 – Thu, May 21 | Fri, Apr 23 – Thu, Jun 10 | Counted nightly from the second night of Pesach to the eve of Shavuot (Leviticus 23:15–16) |
| **The Three Weeks** | Thu, Jul 2 – Thu, Jul 23 | Thu, Jul 22 – Thu, Aug 12 | From the Seventeenth of Tammuz to Tisha B'Av; mourning for the fall of Jerusalem |
| **The Nine Days** | Wed, Jul 15 – Thu, Jul 23 | Wed, Aug 4 – Thu, Aug 12 | From 1 Av; the mourning intensifies |
| **Month of Elul** | Fri, Aug 14 – Fri, Sep 11 | Fri, Sep 3 – Fri, Oct 1 | The month of preparation before Rosh Hashanah; the shofar is sounded each weekday morning |
| **The Days of Awe** | Sat, Sep 12 – Mon, Sep 21 | Sat, Oct 2 – Mon, Oct 11 | The ten days from Rosh Hashanah through Yom Kippur |

**Selichot**, the penitential prayers before Rosh Hashanah, begin at different points by community. In the Ashkenazi custom they begin on the Saturday night before Rosh Hashanah — the night of **Saturday, September 5, 2026** and the night of **Saturday, September 25, 2027**. In most Sephardi and Mizrahi communities they begin at the start of Elul — **August 14, 2026** and **September 3, 2027**.

### The Festivals Appointed in the Torah

Leviticus 23 is the master list — *"Concerning the feasts of the LORD, which ye shall proclaim to be holy convocations, even these are my feasts"* (Leviticus 23:2). Three of them are pilgrimage festivals, the *shalosh regalim*, at which Israel was to appear before the LORD: *"in the feast of unleavened bread, and in the feast of weeks, and in the feast of tabernacles"* (Deuteronomy 16:16; cf. Exodus 23:14).

| Holiday | 2026 | 2027 | In Scripture |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pesach / Passover** — the exodus from Egypt; unleavened bread for seven days; the seder on the first night (and the second night in the Diaspora) | Apr 2–9 | Apr 22–29 | Exodus 12; Leviticus 23:5–8; Deuteronomy 16:1–8 |
| **Shavuot / Pentecost** — the wheat harvest and the giving of the Torah at Sinai; seven weeks after Passover | May 22–23 | Jun 11–12 | Leviticus 23:15–21; Deuteronomy 16:9–12; Acts 2:1 |
| **Rosh Hashanah** — the new year; *"a memorial of blowing of trumpets, an holy convocation"* | Sep 12–13 | Oct 2–3 | Leviticus 23:23–25; Numbers 29:1–6 |
| **Yom Kippur** — the Day of Atonement; a complete fast, *"ye shall afflict your souls, and do no work at all"* | Sep 21 | Oct 11 | Leviticus 16; Leviticus 23:26–32 |
| **Sukkot / Tabernacles** — seven days dwelling in booths, recalling the wilderness; the four species | Sep 26 – Oct 2 | Oct 16–22 | Leviticus 23:33–43; Deuteronomy 16:13–15 |
| **Shemini Atzeret** — *"on the eighth day shall be an holy convocation... it is a solemn assembly"*; the prayer for rain | Oct 3 | Oct 23 | Leviticus 23:36; Numbers 29:35 |

**Hoshana Rabbah** (Oct 2, 2026; Oct 22, 2027) is the seventh and last day of Sukkot, marked by seven circuits with the four species and, in tradition, the final sealing of the year's judgment.

**Simchat Torah** (Oct 4, 2026; Oct 24, 2027) is not named in the Torah. It is the day the annual cycle of Torah readings ends and immediately begins again — the last verses of Deuteronomy and the first of Genesis read one after the other. In Israel it is folded into Shemini Atzeret.

**Passover's seder nights**, for planning: the first seder falls on the evening of **Wednesday, April 1, 2026** and **Wednesday, April 21, 2027**. In the Diaspora a second seder follows the next evening — Thursday, April 2, 2026 and Thursday, April 22, 2027.

### The Fast Days

Four of these mourn the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple, and are the four Zechariah names when he promises they will one day be reversed: *"The fast of the fourth month, and the fast of the fifth, and the fast of the seventh, and the fast of the tenth, shall be to the house of Judah joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts"* (Zechariah 8:19).

| Fast | 2026 | 2027 | What it marks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Asara B'Tevet** (Tenth of Tevet) | Sun, Dec 20 | *none — Jan 9, 2028* | Nebuchadnezzar's siege of Jerusalem begins (Zechariah's "fast of the tenth") |
| **Ta'anit Esther** (Fast of Esther) | Mon, Mar 2 | Mon, Mar 22 | The fast before Purim, after Esther 4:16 |
| **Ta'anit Bechorot** (Fast of the Firstborn) | Wed, Apr 1 | Wed, Apr 21 | Firstborn sons fast on the eve of Passover, for their deliverance in the tenth plague |
| **Shiva Asar B'Tammuz** (Seventeenth of Tammuz) | Thu, Jul 2 | Thu, Jul 22 | The walls of Jerusalem are breached (the "fast of the fourth") |
| **Tisha B'Av** (Ninth of Av) | Thu, Jul 23 | Thu, Aug 12 | Both Temples destroyed; a full fast, the gravest of these (the "fast of the fifth") |
| **Tzom Gedaliah** (Fast of Gedaliah) | Mon, Sep 14 | Mon, Oct 4 | The assassination of Gedaliah, last governor of Judah (2 Kings 25:25; Jeremiah 41) — the "fast of the seventh" |

**Yom Kippur** is also a fast, and the only fast the Torah itself commands (Leviticus 16:29, 23:27). All the fasts above except Yom Kippur and Tisha B'Av run from dawn to nightfall; those two run from sundown to nightfall the following day.

The minor fasts are never kept on Shabbat. Where a fast's Hebrew date would fall on Shabbat it is postponed to Sunday — or, in the case of the Fast of Esther and the Fast of the Firstborn, moved *earlier* to the preceding Thursday, since Purim and Passover eve follow immediately. In 2026 and 2027 no such adjustment is needed: every fast listed falls on its ordinary Hebrew date.

### The Two Rabbinic Festivals

Neither is commanded in the Torah; both are established in the books that record the events they commemorate, and both are kept for the same number of days in Israel and the Diaspora.

**Purim** — Tuesday, March 3, 2026 and Tuesday, March 23, 2027. The deliverance of the Jews of Persia in the book of Esther, which itself institutes the festival: *"that they should keep the fourteenth day of the month Adar, and the fifteenth day of the same, yearly"* (Esther 9:21, 9:31). The Megillah is read on the eve and again in the morning; gifts of food to friends and gifts to the poor are part of the day.

**Shushan Purim** — the day after, March 4, 2026 and March 24, 2027 — is Purim as kept in Jerusalem and other cities walled since the days of Joshua.

**Chanukah** — Dec 5–12, 2026 and Dec 25, 2027 – Jan 1, 2028. Eight days for the rededication of the Temple after the Maccabean revolt, one candle added each night. It is the feast John records Jesus attending: *"And it was at Jerusalem the feast of the dedication, and it was winter"* (John 10:22).

## Appendix B — The Hebrew Years Behind These Dates

Three Hebrew years touch the two Gregorian ones:

| Hebrew year | Runs from | Runs to | Length | Leap? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **5786** | Tue, Sep 23, 2025 | Fri, Sep 11, 2026 | 354 days (regular) | No |
| **5787** | Sat, Sep 12, 2026 | Fri, Oct 1, 2027 | 385 days (complete) | **Yes** |
| **5788** | Sat, Oct 2, 2027 | Wed, Sep 20, 2028 | 355 days (complete) | No |

Each year begins at sundown on the evening before the date shown.

### Why 5787 is thirty-one days longer

Twelve lunar months come to about 354 days, eleven short of a solar year. Left alone the festivals would drift backwards through the seasons — Passover, which the Torah fixes to *"the month of Abib"*, the month of ripening barley (Deuteronomy 16:1), would within a few decades fall in winter. The calendar corrects this by inserting a thirteenth month, **Adar I**, seven times in every nineteen years: in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17, and 19 of the cycle. 5787 is year 11 and so is a leap year; 5786 is year 10 and 5788 is year 12, and both are ordinary.

In a leap year the added month comes before the regular Adar, which is then called **Adar II**. Purim and everything attached to it are kept in Adar II, the month adjacent to Nisan — which is why Purim 2027 falls on March 23 rather than in February, and why Passover 2027 comes three weeks later than Passover 2026.

Hebrew years also vary in length by a day or two according to the day of the week on which Rosh Hashanah may fall, by lengthening or shortening Heshvan and Kislev. A year is **deficient** (Heshvan 29, Kislev 29), **regular** (Heshvan 29, Kislev 30), or **complete** (both 30). 5786 is regular; 5787 and 5788 are both complete.

## Appendix C — How These Dates Were Calculated

Every date on this page was computed, not copied. The calculation implements the **fixed arithmetic calendar** — the calendar attributed to Hillel II in the fourth century and standard in Jewish practice since — as formalised by Dershowitz and Reingold in *Calendrical Calculations* and implemented in GNU Emacs's `cal-hebrew`.

The method, in outline:

1. **Find the molad.** For a given Hebrew year, count the months elapsed since the epoch and multiply by the mean lunation of 29 days, 12 hours, 793 *chalakim* (a *chelek* is 1/1080 of an hour). This gives the mean conjunction — the *molad* — of Tishri.
2. **Apply the four postponements** (*dechiyot*), which shift Rosh Hashanah by a day where needed:
   - *Lo ADU rosh* — Rosh Hashanah never falls on Sunday, Wednesday, or Friday.
   - *Molad zaken* — if the molad is at or after noon, postpone a day.
   - *GaTaRaD* — in a common year, a molad on Tuesday at or after 9h 204p postpones two days.
   - *BeTUTaKaPoT* — in the year after a leap year, a molad on Monday at or after 15h 589p postpones a day.
3. **Set the year's length** from the interval between one Rosh Hashanah and the next, which fixes whether Heshvan and Kislev have 29 or 30 days.
4. **Count from 1 Tishri** to each Hebrew date, and convert the resulting day number to the Gregorian calendar.
5. **Apply each holiday's own rules** — a fast that falls on Shabbat postponed to Sunday or advanced to Thursday, the modern Israeli days shifted away from Shabbat.

The implementation was checked against nine independently known dates before being used — Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur 5784, Rosh Hashanah 5785 and 5786, Passover 2024 and 2025, Chanukah 2024 and 2025, Shavuot 2025 — and reproduced all nine exactly.

### What this calendar is not

The fixed calendar is an arithmetic approximation to the moon, not an observation of it. In the Temple period the new month was declared by the Sanhedrin on the testimony of witnesses who had seen the new crescent, and the mean molad used here can differ from the true conjunction by several hours. Karaite Jews and a few other communities still fix their months by observation, so their festival dates can fall a day away from those listed here. Everything on this page follows the fixed calendar, which is what virtually every printed Jewish calendar, synagogue, and community uses.

Sundown times are not calculated here at all — they depend on where you are standing. For candle-lighting and havdalah times, consult a local calendar.

---

*All dates are given in the Gregorian calendar. Holidays begin at sundown on the evening preceding the date listed.*
