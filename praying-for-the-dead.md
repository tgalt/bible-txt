# Praying for the Dead
## What Scripture Says, What the Church Has Done With It, and What a Grieving Christian Can Do

---

## How to Use This Document

This document answers three questions, in order:

1. **What does the Bible actually say about praying for the dead?** — every passage that bears on it, quoted and read in context, including the one text that says it outright and the reasons most Protestant Bibles do not contain that text (Part I)
2. **How did Christians come to pray for the dead, and who objected?** — from the catacombs to the Reformation to today, with the actual words of the people on each side (Part II)
3. **What can a Christian do with it?** — what is certainly right to pray after someone dies, what the traditions add or refuse, and what to do with the urge itself (Part III)

Part I is the reference material. Part II explains why the question divides Christians along one of the cleanest lines in the church — Catholics and Orthodox pray for the dead; most Protestants do not — and why the line runs where it does. Part III is the practical payoff.

**If you are reading this because someone has died,** go to Part III first. Everything there is either agreed by every Christian tradition or clearly labelled as belonging to one of them, and none of it requires you to settle the argument in Parts I and II before you pray.

### The source of the quotations

Scripture is quoted from the **King James Version (1769)**, the text carried in [`kjv/`](kjv) in this repository. Every quotation was taken directly from that file, so any verse can be checked at its source:

```sh
grep '^2 Samuel 12:23 ' kjv/kjv.txt
```

Where the KJV's English has drifted far enough to mislead, the modern sense is given in brackets or in the surrounding comment — *hell* in the KJV usually translates Hebrew *sheol* or Greek *hades*, the place of the dead generally, not the place of final punishment; *the grave* translates the same Hebrew word; *ought* means anything; *sometime* means formerly.

**The Apocrypha.** The single passage of the Bible that speaks directly of praying for the dead is in 2 Maccabees, a book the 1611 King James Bible printed between the Testaments but which the 66-book text in [`kjv/`](kjv) does not include (see [`kjv/README.md`](kjv/README.md)). It is quoted here, together with Tobit, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Wisdom, and Baruch, from the 1611 KJV Apocrypha as transcribed in the CrossWire OSIS edition that `kjv/README.md` names as its verification source. Those quotations cannot be checked against this repository and are marked *(Apocrypha)* wherever they appear.

### A note on what this document is not

This is a study of the biblical and historical material, not a ruling from a church. On this question the churches have ruled, in opposite directions, and the rulings are quoted. Where sincere Christians have disagreed for fifteen hundred years, the disagreement is laid out rather than settled, and the passages that drive each side are quoted so you can weigh them. Where this document does take a position, it says so and shows its work.

# Part I — What the Bible Says

## Four things that get mixed together

Arguments about praying for the dead go wrong most often at the first step, by treating several different things as one. Scripture speaks to each of them, and what it says about one does not carry over to another.

**1. Praying *to* the dead, or consulting them.** Asking a dead person for help, information, or intercession. The Old Testament forbids consulting the dead in the strongest terms (Deuteronomy 18:10–12; Isaiah 8:19; 1 Samuel 28). Whether the saints in heaven may be *asked to pray* is a separate and large question, not treated here. What matters for this document is that texts forbidding traffic *with* the dead are sometimes quoted against prayer *for* the dead, and they do not reach it. Prayer for the dead is addressed to God.

**2. The prayers *of* the dead.** The souls under the altar cry out for justice (Revelation 6:9–11); the rich man in torment pleads for his brothers (Luke 16:27–28); in 2 Maccabees 15:12–14 *(Apocrypha)* the dead high priest Onias and the prophet Jeremiah are seen praying for the people. These show the dead as conscious and active before God. They say nothing about whether the living can do anything for them, and texts about the prayers of the dead are sometimes cited as if they did.

**3. Praying *about* the dead.** Thanking God for a person's life, remembering them before him, praying for those who mourn them. No Christian tradition objects to any of this, and the strictest Protestant confessions do it in their funeral rites.

**4. Praying *for* the dead.** Asking God to do something for a person who has died — give them rest, light, peace, mercy, forgiveness, a share in the resurrection. This is the question. It has a narrow form (the bare prayer, "Lord, have mercy on him") and an elaborate form (the Mass offered for a soul in purgatory), and the two have very different histories.

There is also a fifth thing, which Scripture records and which is easy to overlook: **praying for a dead person to be restored to life.** Elijah, Elisha, Jesus, and Peter all did it, and God answered. It is treated below in its own section, because it is literally prayer for the dead and yet belongs to a different category from everything else here.

## The words themselves

There is no word in biblical Hebrew or Greek for "prayer for the dead," and no verse that uses such a phrase except 2 Maccabees 12:44 *(Apocrypha)*. What Scripture has instead is a vocabulary of death itself, and the words matter.

**Sheol and Hades.** Hebrew *sheol* is the place of the dead — all the dead, righteous and wicked, in the Old Testament's view. The KJV translates it *grave* (31 times), *hell* (31 times), and *pit* (3 times), and the choice was often made by the translators according to whether a righteous or a wicked person was in view. Its Greek equivalent is *hades*, which the KJV also renders *hell*. This matters most in Luke 16:23, where the rich man is "in hell" — that is, in *hades*, the intermediate state, not in *gehenna*, the lake of fire of final judgment. The story is therefore set in precisely the period that prayer for the dead is meant to address, which is why both sides quote it.

**Gathered to his people.** The oldest phrase.

> Genesis 25:8 — Then Abraham gave up the ghost, and died in a good old age, an old man, and full of years; and was gathered to his people.

**Sleep.** The euphemism both Testaments use for the death of the faithful, and a deliberate one: sleep is a state one wakes from.

> Daniel 12:2 — And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt.

> John 11:11 — These things said he: and after that he saith unto them, Our friend Lazarus sleepeth; but I go, that I may awake him out of sleep.

> Acts 7:60 — And he kneeled down, and cried with a loud voice, Lord, lay not this sin to their charge. And when he had said this, he fell asleep.

> 1 Thessalonians 4:13 — But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope.

The early Christians took the word seriously enough to name their burial grounds from it: *koimeterion*, "sleeping place," is the origin of *cemetery*. The vocabulary carries an expectation — the dead are waiting for something — and the argument about praying for them is, at bottom, an argument about whether anything a living person asks can bear on the waiting.

## Israel's dead: mourning without petition

The Old Testament is full of death and of grief for the dead. It records mourning periods, funeral fasts, laments, funeral meals, and burial customs in detail. What it does not record, anywhere, is a prayer addressed to God on behalf of a person who has died.

> Genesis 50:10 — And they came to the threshingfloor of Atad, which is beyond Jordan, and there they mourned with a great and very sore lamentation: and he made a mourning for his father seven days.

> Deuteronomy 34:8 — And the children of Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab thirty days: so the days of weeping and mourning for Moses were ended.

> 2 Samuel 1:12 — And they mourned, and wept, and fasted until even, for Saul, and for Jonathan his son, and for the people of the LORD, and for the house of Israel; because they were fallen by the sword.

Jeremiah, announcing a judgment so total that the ordinary decencies will lapse, lists what those decencies were:

> Jeremiah 16:6–7 — Both the great and the small shall die in this land: they shall not be buried, neither shall men lament for them, nor cut themselves, nor make themselves bald for them: Neither shall men tear themselves for them in mourning, to comfort them for the dead; neither shall men give them the cup of consolation to drink for their father or for their mother.

Burial, lament, gestures of grief, and a meal of consolation for the mourners. Every item is directed at the living or at the body. The "cup of consolation" is given *to the bereaved*, "for" — that is, on account of — their father or mother. Nothing in the list is aimed at the state of the dead person before God. The same is true of the New Testament's one funeral notice:

> Acts 8:2 — And devout men carried Stephen to his burial, and made great lamentation over him.

This is the baseline. The whole Bible's account of what the people of God did when someone died is: they wept, they fasted, they buried, they comforted one another. Whether the absence of anything else is a silence or a verdict is what the rest of Part I is about.

The Old Testament does know the ache that drives the question. David's cry over Absalom is the wish every bereaved parent has had, and it is the closest thing in Scripture to a prayer for a dead child — and it is addressed to no one:

> 2 Samuel 18:33 — And the king was much moved, and went up to the chamber over the gate, and wept: and as he went, thus he said, O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!

## David and the child

The passage most often quoted on the Protestant side, and rightly, because it is the one place in Scripture where someone explains why he stopped praying when a person died.

> 2 Samuel 12:16 — David therefore besought God for the child; and David fasted, and went in, and lay all night upon the earth.

> 2 Samuel 12:20–23 — Then David arose from the earth, and washed, and anointed himself, and changed his apparel, and came into the house of the LORD, and worshipped: then he came to his own house; and when he required, they set bread before him, and he did eat. Then said his servants unto him, What thing is this that thou hast done? thou didst fast and weep for the child, while it was alive; but when the child was dead, thou didst rise and eat bread. And he said, While the child was yet alive, I fasted and wept: for I said, Who can tell whether GOD will be gracious to me, that the child may live? But now he is dead, wherefore should I fast? can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me.

**What it shows.** David prayed and fasted with everything he had while there was something to ask for. When the child died, he stopped — not from despair, but because the thing he had been asking for was no longer possible, and he says so: *can I bring him back again?* He then worships. His confidence about the child is expressed not as a petition but as a statement: *I shall go to him.* This is the pattern of a man for whom death has closed the question and opened a different one — his own.

**What it does not show.** David's prayer was for the child's *life*. Once that was refused, the specific petition was moot, and his reasoning is about that petition. He does not say "there is nothing to pray for the dead"; he says he cannot bring the child back. Those who pray for the dead point out that no one has ever prayed for the dead in order to bring them back.

**A fair reading.** The passage is not a proof text against prayer for the dead, but it is a clear picture of a godly man's instinct: petition belongs to the living, and once someone has died the right response is worship and the confidence that God has them. That instinct is the Old Testament's whole practice, and David voices it. It is also, notably, the instinct of the Apocrypha's own wisdom teacher:

> Sirach 38:21 *(Apocrypha)* — Forget it not, for there is no turning again: thou shalt not do him good, but hurt thyself.

> Sirach 38:23 *(Apocrypha)* — When the dead is at rest, let his remembrance rest; and be comforted for him, when his Spirit is departed from him.

The same collection of books that contains 2 Maccabees 12 contains this. The Jewish tradition was not of one mind.

## The Law's one word on offerings for the dead

The Law of Moses never addresses prayer for the dead, in either direction. It comes closest in the confession the Israelite made when bringing the third-year tithe:

> Deuteronomy 26:14 — I have not eaten thereof in my mourning, neither have I taken away ought thereof for any unclean use, nor given ought thereof for the dead: but I have hearkened to the voice of the LORD my God, and have done according to all that thou hast commanded me.

The tithe was holy; it could not be eaten by a mourner (who was ritually unclean by contact with a corpse), nor used for anything unclean, nor "given for the dead." The last phrase is best explained by Israel's neighbours. Egyptian tombs were stocked with food for the dead; Canaanite religion had a cult of the dead with offerings and funerary meals. The psalmist's description of Israel's apostasy at Baal-peor uses the same idea:

> Psalms 106:28 — They joined themselves also unto Baalpeor, and ate the sacrifices of the dead.

("The dead" here may mean lifeless idols, or the cult of the dead, or both.) What Deuteronomy 26:14 forbids is diverting the holy tithe to the dead — feeding or provisioning them as the pagans did. It says nothing about asking God for mercy on them. But it does show the Law's posture: the dead were not to be a destination for Israel's offerings, and the one time offerings for the dead are mentioned, it is to disavow them.

## The wall between the living and the dead

> Deuteronomy 18:10–12 — There shall not be found among you any one that maketh his son or his daughter to pass through the fire, or that useth divination, or an observer of times, or an enchanter, or a witch. Or a charmer, or a consulter with familiar spirits, or a wizard, or a necromancer. For all that do these things are an abomination unto the LORD: and because of these abominations the LORD thy God doth drive them out from before thee.

> Isaiah 8:19–20 — And when they shall say unto you, Seek unto them that have familiar spirits, and unto wizards that peep, and that mutter: should not a people seek unto their God? for the living to the dead? To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them.

Saul's visit to the medium at Endor is the narrative form of the same prohibition, and it is worth noticing what the dead prophet says when he appears:

> 1 Samuel 28:15 — And Samuel said to Saul, Why hast thou disquieted me, to bring me up? And Saul answered, I am sore distressed; for the Philistines make war against me, and God is departed from me, and answereth me no more, neither by prophets, nor by dreams: therefore I have called thee, that thou mayest make known unto me what I shall do.

The dead are at rest and are not to be disturbed; the living are to seek God, not them. This is a real and heavy wall, and any practice that climbs it — spiritualism, séances, "messages" from the departed — is condemned without qualification.

**But it is the wrong wall for this question.** Prayer for the dead does not consult the dead, disturb them, or seek anything from them. It asks God. Isaiah's rebuke is that the people sought the dead *instead of* God; prayer for the dead, whatever else may be said of it, seeks God. These verses are decisive against necromancy and against nothing else, and a Protestant case that leans on them is leaning on the wrong texts. The better Protestant texts come later.

## What the dead can and cannot do

A cluster of passages describes the dead as beyond activity, praise, and knowledge of the world.

> Psalms 6:5 — For in death there is no remembrance of thee: in the grave who shall give thee thanks?

> Psalms 88:10–12 — Wilt thou shew wonders to the dead? shall the dead arise and praise thee? Selah. Shall thy lovingkindness be declared in the grave? or thy faithfulness in destruction? Shall thy wonders be known in the dark? and thy righteousness in the land of forgetfulness?

> Psalms 115:17 — The dead praise not the LORD, neither any that go down into silence.

> Isaiah 38:18–19 — For the grave cannot praise thee, death can not celebrate thee: they that go down into the pit cannot hope for thy truth. The living, the living, he shall praise thee, as I do this day: the father to the children shall make known thy truth.

> Ecclesiastes 9:5–6 — For the living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten. Also their love, and their hatred, and their envy, is now perished; neither have they any more a portion for ever in any thing that is done under the sun.

> Ecclesiastes 9:10 — Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.

> Job 7:9–10 — As the cloud is consumed and vanisheth away: so he that goeth down to the grave shall come up no more. He shall return no more to his house, neither shall his place know him any more.

Three things need saying about these.

**First, they describe the dead from the earthly side.** Their subject is what the dead can no longer do — praise in the assembly, know what happens under the sun, work, return home. They do not say what God can or cannot do for them. Ecclesiastes 9:10 is the strongest: there is no *work* in the grave, so whatever is to be done must be done now. That is a genuine argument that the dead cannot change their own state. It is not, strictly, an argument about what the living may ask.

**Second, they were written before the hope of resurrection was clear.** The Old Testament's view of what lies past death is dim by design, and it brightens over time. The same book of Job that says the dead come up no more also says:

> Job 19:25–26 — For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth: And though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God:

And Daniel 12:2, quoted above, is explicit about waking. Jesus himself read the Old Testament as teaching that the dead are alive to God:

> Luke 20:37–38 — Now that the dead are raised, even Moses shewed at the bush, when he calleth the Lord the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. For he is not a God of the dead, but of the living: for all live unto him.

So these psalms cannot be pressed into saying the dead are simply gone. They say the dead are silent *here*.

**Third, if they prove anything about prayer, they cut hardest against the invocation of the saints,** since a dead person who "knows not any thing" of what happens under the sun is not obviously in a position to be asked for help. That is a different debate, but it is why Protestants who use these verses should use them carefully.

## No ransom for a brother

> Psalms 49:7–9 — None of them can by any means redeem his brother, nor give to God a ransom for him: (For the redemption of their soul is precious, and it ceaseth for ever:) That he should still live for ever, and not see corruption.

> Ezekiel 18:20 — The soul that sinneth, it shall die. The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son: the righteousness of the righteous shall be upon him, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon him.

Each person answers for their own life, and no one can pay for another. This is the principle behind the Protestant objection to Masses purchased for the dead: the notion that one person's act — a payment, an offering, a pilgrimage — can be credited to another's account.

But the principle has to be applied with care, because Scripture also shows a father offering sacrifice for his sons' possible sins, and being commended for it:

> Job 1:5 — And it was so, when the days of their feasting were gone about, that Job sent and sanctified them, and rose up early in the morning, and offered burnt offerings according to the number of them all: for Job said, It may be that my sons have sinned, and cursed God in their hearts. Thus did Job continually.

Job's sons were alive. But the logic — that one person may bring another's sin before God and ask mercy for it — is intercession, and Scripture is full of it. Moses for Israel, Abraham for Sodom, Paul for his churches, "pray one for another" (James 5:16). Psalm 49 forbids *ransom*, a payment that purchases another's life. It does not forbid asking. The question is whether asking has any object once the person has died, and Psalm 49 does not answer that. (John Chrysostom, in the fourth century, used Job 1:5 as his main argument that offerings for the dead do them good; see Part II.)

## The prayers Scripture does record for the dead

Four times in Scripture someone prays over a dead body, and four times the dead person gets up.

> 1 Kings 17:20–22 — And he cried unto the LORD, and said, O LORD my God, hast thou also brought evil upon the widow with whom I sojourn, by slaying her son? And he stretched himself upon the child three times, and cried unto the LORD, and said, O LORD my God, I pray thee, let this child's soul come into him again. And the LORD heard the voice of Elijah; and the soul of the child came into him again, and he revived.

> 2 Kings 4:32–33 — And when Elisha was come into the house, behold, the child was dead, and laid upon his bed. He went in therefore, and shut the door upon them twain, and prayed unto the LORD.

> John 11:41–43 — Then they took away the stone from the place where the dead was laid. And Jesus lifted up his eyes, and said, Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me. And I knew that thou hearest me always: but because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe that thou hast sent me. And when he thus had spoken, he cried with a loud voice, Lazarus, come forth.

> Acts 9:40 — But Peter put them all forth, and kneeled down, and prayed; and turning him to the body said, Tabitha, arise. And she opened her eyes: and when she saw Peter, she sat up.

Elijah's prayer is, word for word, a prayer for a dead person: *let this child's soul come into him again.* God heard it. These passages settle one thing that is sometimes asserted too quickly — that death places a person entirely outside the reach of any prayer. It does not. God raised the dead in answer to prayer.

But they settle only that. Every one of these prayers asked for return to earthly life, and every one was a sign given through a prophet, the Lord, or an apostle. None was a regular practice. None asked anything about the dead person's state before God. And no one in Scripture, or in the early church, cited them as precedent for prayer about the dead's condition beyond this life. They are the exception that shows where the rule lies: when Scripture records prayer for the dead, it is prayer to have them back.

## Between the Testaments: the one passage that says it outright

### 2 Maccabees 12

2 Maccabees is a Greek abridgment of a lost five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene (2 Maccabees 2:23), written in the late second century BC and covering the Maccabean revolt of the 160s. In chapter 12, after a battle near Adullam (KJV: Odollam), Judas Maccabeus and his men gather their dead for burial and discover why they fell:

> 2 Maccabees 12:39–45 *(Apocrypha)* — And upon the day following, as the use had been, Judas and his company came to take up the bodies of them that were slain, and to bury them with their kinsmen in their fathers' graves. Now under the coats of every one that was slain they found things consecrated to the idols of the Jamnites, which is forbidden the Jews by the law. Then every man saw that this was the cause wherefore they were slain. All men therefore praising the Lord, the righteous Judge, who had opened the things that were hid, Betook themselves unto prayer, and besought him that the sin committed might wholly be put out of remembrance. Besides, that noble Judas exhorted the people to keep themselves from sin, forsomuch as they saw before their eyes the things that came to pass for the sins of those that were slain. And when he had made a gathering throughout the company to the sum of two thousand drachms of silver, he sent it to Jerusalem to offer a sin offering, doing therein very well and honestly, in that he was mindful of the resurrection: For if he had not hoped that they that were slain should have risen again, it had been superfluous and vain to pray for the dead. And also in that he perceived that there was great favour laid up for those that died godly, it was an holy and good thought. Whereupon he made a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin.

This is the passage. It is the only place in any Bible where prayer for the dead is described, defended, and commended in so many words. Five things are in it:

1. **The dead had died in sin.** Pagan amulets under their tunics — idolatry, the sin the Law names first.
2. **The living prayed that the sin be blotted out** — a prayer to God, for the dead, about their sin.
3. **A sin offering was sent to the Temple** on their behalf, paid for by a collection.
4. **The whole thing rested on resurrection.** The author's argument is explicit: unless the dead rise, praying for them is "superfluous and vain." Prayer for the dead here is a corollary of resurrection hope, not of a doctrine of the soul.
5. **The narrator calls it "an holy and good thought"** and says Judas "made a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin."

### Why it does not settle the matter

**For those who accept the book,** the passage still fits awkwardly with the doctrine it is usually cited for. The men died in idolatry, which Catholic theology classes as mortal sin; purgatory, in that theology, is for those who die in grace with lesser faults. Catholic commentators have long acknowledged this and generally take the passage as proof of the *practice* — that Jews prayed for the dead and Scripture approves — rather than as a description of purgatory.

**For those who do not,** the book is not canonical, and it does not claim to be. Its author closes by hoping he has written well and apologising if he has not:

> 2 Maccabees 15:38 *(Apocrypha)* — And if I have done well, and as is fitting the story, it is that which I desired: but if slenderly and meanly, it is that which I could attain unto.

No canonical writer speaks of his own work that way. Jerome, who translated the Latin Bible, held that the Apocrypha were to be read for edification but not "to confirm the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas." The Church of England put the same principle in its Sixth Article (1571): the church reads them "for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine." The Westminster Confession (1646) excludes them from the canon altogether. Rome, at the Council of Trent in 1546, defined them as Scripture, and the Orthodox churches receive them with varying degrees of authority.

**Whichever way the canon question goes, the passage establishes a historical fact:** by the second century BC, devout Jews prayed and offered sacrifice for their dead, and thought it holy. The Jewish world Jesus and the apostles lived in knew the practice. That means the New Testament's silence on the subject is silence in a setting where the practice existed, and both sides draw opposite conclusions from that (see below).

### The other Apocryphal passages

Three more verses show what surrounded the practice, and one is regularly quoted *against* purgatory.

> Tobit 4:17 *(Apocrypha)* — Pour out thy bread on the burial of the just, but give nothing to the wicked.

> Sirach 7:33 *(Apocrypha)* — A gift hath grace in the sight of every man living; and for the dead detain it not.

These reflect gifts and funeral provisions made "for the dead" — probably alms and food for the mourners' meal, possibly offerings at the grave — and show the Jewish instinct that something is owed to the dead. They are not prayers.

> Baruch 3:4–5 *(Apocrypha)* — O Lord Almighty, thou God of Israel, hear now the prayers of the dead Israelites, and of their children, which have sinned before thee, and not hearkened unto the voice of thee their God: for the which cause these plagues cleave unto us. Remember not the iniquities of our forefathers: but think upon thy power and thy name now at this time.

Sometimes cited for prayer *for* the dead, this is a prayer *of* the dead (or of Israel as figuratively dead in exile). It asks God to remember the prayers of the fathers, not to do something for them.

> Wisdom 3:1–3 *(Apocrypha)* — But the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and there shall no torment touch them. In the sight of the unwise they seemed to die: and their departure is taken for misery, And their going from us to be utter destruction: but they are in peace.

The righteous dead are in God's hand, untouched by torment, at peace. This is the Apocrypha's clearest statement about the state of the faithful dead, and it is the Protestant picture.

## The New Testament

### Silence where you would expect a word

The New Testament contains no command to pray for the dead, no example of anyone doing so, and no prohibition of it. The silence is heavier than it sounds, because the New Testament speaks at length about the very situations in which such prayer would arise.

**Paul's pastoral teaching on bereavement** is a full paragraph, and it says what to do with grief:

> 1 Thessalonians 4:13–14 — But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again, even so them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him.

> 1 Thessalonians 4:16–18 — For the Lord himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first: Then we which are alive and remain shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air: and so shall we ever be with the Lord. Wherefore comfort one another with these words.

The comfort offered is the resurrection. The instruction is to comfort one another. There is no "and pray for them."

**Paul's instruction on the scope of prayer** lists who is to be prayed for:

> 1 Timothy 2:1–2 — I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men; For kings, and for all that are in authority; that we may lead a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty.

"All men" — and the examples are the living. (Those who pray for the dead sometimes ask whether the dead have stopped being men. It is a fair question and not a proof.)

**1 Corinthians 15,** the longest treatment of death and resurrection in the Bible, mentions something done "for the dead" exactly once (verse 29, below) and never prayer.

**Jesus' teaching on prayer** — the Lord's Prayer, the parables of persistence, the promises about asking — has nothing about the dead.

The argument from silence runs both ways, and honesty requires saying so. **The Protestant reading:** a practice this significant, in documents this concerned with death, prayer, and the errors of the churches, would have been commanded, modelled, or at least mentioned if it were apostolic; the New Testament mentions many smaller things. **The Catholic and Orthodox reading:** the practice was so ordinary a part of Jewish piety that no one needed to mention it, and the New Testament assumes a great deal that it never spells out — the order of synagogue worship, the form of the Lord's Day, the way baptisms were done. Neither is a proof. What can be said is that the New Testament nowhere *teaches* prayer for the dead, and that everyone agrees.

### Texts cited in favour

**1. Onesiphorus (2 Timothy 1:16–18; 4:19).** The nearest thing to a New Testament prayer for a dead person, and genuinely disputed.

> 2 Timothy 1:16–18 — The Lord give mercy unto the house of Onesiphorus; for he oft refreshed me, and was not ashamed of my chain: But, when he was in Rome, he sought me out very diligently, and found me. The Lord grant unto him that he may find mercy of the Lord in that day: and in how many things he ministered unto me at Ephesus, thou knowest very well.

> 2 Timothy 4:19 — Salute Prisca and Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus.

The case that Onesiphorus was dead: Paul prays for his *house* (1:16) and greets his *household* (4:19) but never greets the man himself, though he is plainly thinking of him; the past tenses ("he oft refreshed me," "he ministered") sound like an obituary; and the prayer for him is deferred to "that day," the day of judgment, as if nothing nearer could be asked. Many modern commentators, of every confession, think it likely he had died. The case against: Onesiphorus may simply have been away from his household — he had after all travelled to Rome — and Paul greets the family at Ephesus because that is where the letter is going; nothing in the text says he died.

Even if he was dead, what Paul asks is that he "find mercy of the Lord in that day" — mercy at the judgment. He asks nothing about Onesiphorus's present state, nothing about relief, nothing about purification. A Reformed Christian could pray those exact words for a living friend. So the most the passage yields is that Paul may have expressed a wish, in the form of a prayer, for God's mercy on a dead man at the last day. That is something. It is not a doctrine.

**2. Baptism for the dead (1 Corinthians 15:29).**

> 1 Corinthians 15:29 — Else what shall they do which are baptized for the dead, if the dead rise not at all? why are they then baptized for the dead?

This is the one New Testament reference to a rite performed on behalf of the dead, and no one knows what it was. Something like forty interpretations exist. The most natural reading of the Greek is that some at Corinth were being baptised vicariously on behalf of people who had died unbaptised; Chrysostom says the Marcionites did exactly this in his day, and Tertullian knew of it too. Other readings: baptism "on account of" the dead (that is, moved by the witness of dead Christians), baptism "to fill the places of" the dead, baptism "in view of" one's own death. Paul neither endorses nor condemns the practice; he uses it as an argument against the Corinthians' own position — *you* do this, so *you* must believe in resurrection. The verse is not about prayer, and it is the basis of the Latter-day Saints' proxy baptisms, which no other Christian body practises.

**3. "Neither in this world, neither in the world to come" (Matthew 12:32).**

> Matthew 12:32 — And whosoever speaketh a word against the Son of man, it shall be forgiven him: but whosoever speaketh against the Holy Ghost, it shall not be forgiven him, neither in this world, neither in the world to come.

Augustine and Gregory the Great reasoned that if one sin is unforgivable "in the world to come," others must be forgivable there, and this became a main text for purgatory. The parallel in Mark suggests the phrase is simply an idiom for *never*:

> Mark 3:29 — But he that shall blaspheme against the Holy Ghost hath never forgiveness, but is in danger of eternal damnation:

The inference from Matthew is possible but far from necessary, and it is worth noticing that the fathers who drew it did so cautiously.

**4. "Saved; yet so as by fire" (1 Corinthians 3:11–15).**

> 1 Corinthians 3:13–15 — Every man's work shall be made manifest: for the day shall declare it, because it shall be revealed by fire; and the fire shall try every man's work of what sort it is. If any man's work abide which he hath built thereupon, he shall receive a reward. If any man's work shall be burned, he shall suffer loss: but he himself shall be saved; yet so as by fire.

The classic purgatory text, and the context is against it: Paul is speaking of Christian *teachers* whose *work* — what they built on the foundation of Christ — will be tested "in the day," the day of judgment. The fire tests the work, not the person; the person "shall be saved," escaping as a man escapes a burning house. Nothing here is between death and judgment, and nothing is purgative. Even so, a purifying fire was read into the verse from Origen onward, and Augustine allowed that it might be true.

**5. "Till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing" (Matthew 5:25–26).**

> Matthew 5:25–26 — Agree with thine adversary quickly, whiles thou art in the way with him; lest at any time the adversary deliver thee to the judge, and the judge deliver thee to the officer, and thou be cast into prison. Verily I say unto thee, Thou shalt by no means come out thence, till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing.

Tertullian read the prison as a detention after death from which one is released on payment. The plain sense is a warning to reconcile now, while you can, because judgment is coming; "till thou hast paid the uttermost farthing," with no farthing to pay it with, means *never*.

**6. Christ and the spirits in prison (1 Peter 3:18–20; 4:6).**

> 1 Peter 3:18–19 — For Christ also hath once suffered for sins, the just for the unjust, that he might bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh, but quickened by the Spirit: By which also he went and preached unto the spirits in prison;

> 1 Peter 4:6 — For for this cause was the gospel preached also to them that are dead, that they might be judged according to men in the flesh, but live according to God in the spirit.

Among the hardest verses in the New Testament. Whatever the "preaching to the spirits in prison" was — a proclamation of victory to fallen angels, the preaching of Noah in Noah's day by Christ's Spirit, or an announcement to the dead of Noah's generation — it was a single act of Christ, not a pattern for the church, and 4:6 most naturally means that the gospel was preached to people who have since died. At most these verses show that the dead were not beyond Christ's reach at his death. They do not say the living can reach them.

**7. "That they without us should not be made perfect" (Hebrews 11:39–40).**

> Hebrews 11:39–40 — And these all, having obtained a good report through faith, received not the promise: God having provided some better thing for us, that they without us should not be made perfect.

The Old Testament saints wait for their completion until the whole people of God is gathered. The Orthodox draw from this that the state of the departed is not yet final and that the church's prayer belongs to the process; Protestants read the "perfection" as the resurrection, which no prayer hastens.

**8. The dead are alive to God (Luke 20:38; Romans 14:8–9; Romans 8:38–39).**

> Romans 14:8–9 — For whether we live, we live unto the Lord; and whether we die, we die unto the Lord: whether we live therefore, or die, we are the Lord's. For to this end Christ both died, and rose, and revived, that he might be Lord both of the dead and living.

> Romans 8:38–39 — For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.

The dead in Christ remain his; death does not cut them off from the body of Christ or from his love. From this those who pray for the dead argue that the communion of the church spans death, and that a member of Christ's body is never out of range of the body's prayer. Protestants agree with the premise and deny that it implies the practice: the dead in Christ need nothing that prayer could supply, which is precisely what Romans 8 says.

### Texts cited against

**1. The great gulf (Luke 16:19–31).**

> Luke 16:22–26 — And it came to pass, that the beggar died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom: the rich man also died, and was buried; And in hell he lift up his eyes, being in torments, and seeth Abraham afar off, and Lazarus in his bosom. And he cried and said, Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water, and cool my tongue; for I am tormented in this flame. But Abraham said, Son, remember that thou in thy lifetime receivedst thy good things, and likewise Lazarus evil things: but now he is comforted, and thou art tormented. And beside all this, between us and you there is a great gulf fixed: so that they which would pass from hence to you cannot; neither can they pass to us, that would come from thence.

> Luke 16:29–31 — Abraham saith unto him, They have Moses and the prophets; let them hear them. And he said, Nay, father Abraham: but if one went unto them from the dead, they will repent. And he said unto him, If they hear not Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded, though one rose from the dead.

The strongest passage on the Protestant side, for four reasons. The story is set in *hades* (KJV "hell"), the intermediate state, so it speaks to exactly the period in which prayer for the dead would operate. The states of both men are settled: one "is comforted," the other "tormented," and a gulf is "fixed." No one in the story — not the rich man, not Abraham — suggests that anything could alter the rich man's condition, and he does not ask. And the one intercession attempted, from the dead for the living, is refused with the words *they have Moses and the prophets*: the means of salvation are in this life, in the word of God, now.

The rejoinder is that the story concerns a man who died condemned, and no one who prays for the dead thinks prayer can rescue the damned; the passage says nothing about the saved-but-imperfect for whom Catholic and Orthodox prayer is made. That is true. But the passage does establish that the intermediate state has a fixed gulf in it, and that the dead who need something most are past helping.

**2. "To day shalt thou be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:42–43).**

> Luke 23:42–43 — And he said unto Jesus, Lord, remember me when thou comest into thy kingdom. And Jesus said unto him, Verily I say unto thee, To day shalt thou be with me in paradise.

A man with a lifetime of crime and minutes of faith goes straight to Christ, with no purgation and no one to pray for him. The reply that this is an exceptional grace is fair, but it concedes that the ordinary Christian's hope rests on the same grace.

**3. Once to die, then judgment (Hebrews 9:27).**

> Hebrews 9:27 — And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment:

Death, then judgment; nothing in between. It does not say the judgment is immediate, and it does not itself rule out a purification before it, but it leaves no room for a change of destiny.

**4. Absent from the body, present with the Lord (2 Corinthians 5:6–8; Philippians 1:21–23).**

> 2 Corinthians 5:8 — We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord.

> Philippians 1:21, 23 — For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain. … For I am in a strait betwixt two, having a desire to depart, and to be with Christ; which is far better:

Paul expects death to bring him directly to Christ, and calls it gain and "far better." There is no interval of suffering in his expectation. The rejoinder that Paul speaks only of himself and the apostles is weak: he writes to comfort ordinary Christians with the same hope.

**5. The dead in the Lord rest (Revelation 14:13).**

> Revelation 14:13 — And I heard a voice from heaven saying unto me, Write, Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord from henceforth: Yea, saith the Spirit, that they may rest from their labours; and their works do follow them.

Rest, not purgation; and their works follow them, which is to say the judgment is on what was done in life.

**6. Already perfected (Hebrews 10:14; 12:23; 1 John 1:7; Romans 8:1; John 5:24).**

> Hebrews 10:14 — For by one offering he hath perfected for ever them that are sanctified.

> Hebrews 12:22–23 — But ye are come unto mount Sion, and unto the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to an innumerable company of angels, To the general assembly and church of the firstborn, which are written in heaven, and to God the Judge of all, and to the spirits of just men made perfect,

> John 5:24 — Verily, verily, I say unto you, He that heareth my word, and believeth on him that sent me, hath everlasting life, and shall not come into condemnation; but is passed from death unto life.

> Romans 8:1 — There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit.

These are the texts that matter most to the Reformers, and they target purgatory rather than the bare prayer. If Christ's one offering has "perfected for ever" those who are his, if the dead saints are "just men made perfect," if the believer "shall not come into condemnation," then there is no remaining debt for a living person's prayer to help pay. The Catholic answer distinguishes eternal punishment (remitted by Christ) from temporal consequences and remaining imperfection (purged after death); the Protestant reply is that Scripture knows no such division and that "perfected for ever" means what it says. This is the real hinge of the argument — see the next section.

**7. A limit on intercession (1 John 5:16).**

> 1 John 5:16 — If any man see his brother sin a sin which is not unto death, he shall ask, and he shall give him life for them that sin not unto death. There is a sin unto death: I do not say that he shall pray for it.

Prayer for others is commanded and has limits. This does not name death as one of them, but it shows that "pray for everything" is not the New Testament's rule.

**8. Now is the time (John 9:4; 2 Corinthians 6:2; Hebrews 3:13; Ecclesiastes 11:3).**

> John 9:4 — I must work the works of him that sent me, while it is day: the night cometh, when no man can work.

> 2 Corinthians 6:2 — (For he saith, I have heard thee in a time accepted, and in the day of salvation have I succoured thee: behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation.)

> Ecclesiastes 11:3 — If the clouds be full of rain, they empty themselves upon the earth: and if the tree fall toward the south, or toward the north, in the place where the tree falleth, there it shall be.

The consistent note of Scripture is that this life is the time for decision and work, and that where the tree falls it lies. None of these is about prayer for the dead, but together they form the atmosphere in which the New Testament's silence sits.

**9. The secret things (Deuteronomy 29:29).**

> Deuteronomy 29:29 — The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law.

Zwingli's principle, in effect: what God has not revealed about the dead, we should not build on.

### What both sides agree Scripture teaches

It helps to see how much is shared, because the disagreement is narrower than the heat around it suggests.

- **Death does not end the person.** Luke 20:38; Luke 16:22–23; Philippians 1:23.
- **The dead in Christ are with him, at rest, and secure.** 2 Corinthians 5:8; Revelation 14:13; Romans 8:38–39.
- **The judgment is according to what was done in the body.** 2 Corinthians 5:10; Revelation 20:12–13; Romans 14:12.
- **There is no conversion after death.** Luke 16:26; Hebrews 9:27. Catholic and Orthodox teaching is emphatic that prayer for the dead is never for the damned and never to change a destiny.
- **No one is saved by another's works or payment.** Psalm 49:7; Ezekiel 18:20. Rome's own teaching is that the dead cannot merit and that whatever suffrages accomplish, they accomplish by God's mercy.
- **Grief is right, and hopeless grief is wrong.** 1 Thessalonians 4:13; John 11:35.
- **The living should not consult, disturb, or seek the dead.** Deuteronomy 18; Isaiah 8:19.

What remains is one question: **for a person who died in Christ, is there anything God has yet to do for them that a living person's prayer can bear on?** Everything else follows from the answer.

---

## What the question turns on: the state of the dead

The answer depends on what one believes happens between death and the resurrection. There are three main Christian views, and each makes prayer for the dead either pointless, purposeful, or something in between.

| | **View A — Fixed at death** | **View B — Fixed, but purified** | **View C — Awaiting the Judgment** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Held by** | Reformed, most Lutherans, Baptists, most evangelicals | Roman Catholic | Eastern Orthodox |
| **At death, the believer** | Is immediately with Christ, perfected in holiness, awaiting only the body's resurrection | Is judged and assured of heaven, but if imperfectly purified undergoes purification (purgatory) first | Receives a foretaste of their final state; the final verdict is at the Last Judgment |
| **At death, the unbeliever** | Is immediately in torment, awaiting the resurrection to judgment | The same | Receives a foretaste of condemnation; final verdict at the Judgment |
| **What prayer for the dead could do** | Nothing for the believer (already perfect) or the unbeliever (fixed) | Hasten or ease purification | Ask God's mercy on those whose state is not yet sealed |
| **Key texts** | Luke 23:43; 2 Cor 5:8; Phil 1:23; Heb 10:14; 12:23; Rev 14:13; Luke 16:26 | 1 Cor 3:15; Matt 12:32; 5:26; 2 Macc 12; Rev 21:27 (nothing unclean enters) | Heb 11:40; 2 Macc 12; Rom 14:9; the church's practice |
| **Confessional statement** | Westminster Confession 32.1: "Besides these two places, for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none" | Trent, Session 25 (1563); *Catechism* 1030–1032 | No single definition; liturgical practice and the Confession of Dositheus (1672) |

Two observations.

**First, View A still leaves one thing a living person can pray about concerning the dead: the resurrection and the Day.** Even on the strictest Reformed view, the dead in Christ are waiting — for their bodies, for the judgment, for the kingdom in its fullness. The martyrs under the altar are still asking "How long" (Revelation 6:10). When a Christian prays "Thy kingdom come" (Matthew 6:10) or "Even so, come, Lord Jesus" (Revelation 22:20), they are praying for something the dead have not yet received. Some Reformed writers have granted that this is, in the loosest sense, prayer for the dead, and that it is the only kind Scripture models.

**Second, the disagreement about purgatory is not the same as the disagreement about prayer for the dead.** The practice is at least three centuries older than the doctrine (Part II), the Orthodox keep the practice while rejecting the doctrine, and the earliest Reformers rejected the doctrine while tolerating a bare prayer. Someone who is sure purgatory is unbiblical has not thereby settled whether "Lord, have mercy on her" is a permissible thing to say about one's mother. The two questions come apart, and the history shows it.

# Part II — What the Church Has Done

## The first hundred years: silence

The writings of the generation after the apostles — the *Didache*, Clement of Rome, Ignatius, Polycarp, the *Epistle of Barnabas*, Hermas — say nothing about prayer for the dead. Justin Martyr's description of Christian worship (about 155), the earliest we have, mentions prayers for "all men everywhere" and nothing for the dead. Irenaeus (about 180) does not mention it. The silence runs from the death of the apostles to roughly the year 200.

Both sides read it as they read the New Testament's. If the practice were apostolic, the Protestant asks, why does no one in a century of Christian writing mention it? Because, the Catholic answers, no one was asked, and the same writers say nothing about many things the church certainly did. What can be said is that there is no evidence, of any kind, for Christian prayer for the dead before the last decades of the second century.

---

## The earliest witnesses

When the evidence begins, it begins in several places at once, and no one treats the practice as new.

**The *Acts of Paul and Thecla*** (Asia Minor, probably 160–190), a popular and non-canonical romance about Paul's convert Thecla, has her lodging in Antioch with a queen, Tryphaena, whose dead daughter Falconilla appears to her mother in a dream asking that Thecla pray for her, so that she may be "transferred to the place of the just." Thecla prays that Falconilla may "live for ever." It is fiction, but fiction that assumed its readers would find such a prayer natural.

**The epitaph of Abercius** (Hierapolis in Phrygia, about 200), the oldest datable Christian inscription, ends with a request that everyone who reads it pray for Abercius. The catacomb inscriptions of Rome from the late second century onward carry the same instinct in shorter form: *in peace*, *may you live in God*, and requests for *refrigerium* — refreshment — for the departed. Most of these are statements or wishes rather than prayers, but the line between "may she rest in peace" as a wish and as a petition was already thin.

**The *Passion of Perpetua*** (Carthage, 203) is the first Christian narrative of prayer for a dead person and the one that shaped the imagination of the West. Perpetua, in prison awaiting execution, suddenly remembers her brother Dinocrates, who had died at seven of a cancer of the face. She sees him in a vision: in a dark place, hot, thirsty, still bearing his wound, unable to reach the water in a basin whose rim is above his head. She prays for him "day and night, with groans and tears." Some days later she sees him again: clean, well dressed, the wound healed to a scar, the rim of the basin lowered to his waist, drinking from a golden cup and going off to play. She understood, she writes, that he had been released from his suffering. The pattern — a dead child in distress, a living Christian's persistent prayer, relief — is the whole later practice in miniature. (Augustine, two centuries on, was troubled that Dinocrates had apparently died unbaptised and argued that he must have been baptised after all, since otherwise no prayer could have helped him.)

**Tertullian** (Carthage, writing 200–220) is the first theologian to mention the practice, and he mentions it as settled custom. Listing things Christians do that have no scriptural command, he writes: "As often as the anniversary comes round, we make offerings for the dead as birthday honours" (*On the Crown* 3). Elsewhere he says the Christian widow "prays for his soul, and requests refreshment for him meanwhile, and fellowship with him in the first resurrection; and she offers her sacrifice on the anniversaries of his falling asleep" (*On Monogamy* 10). Tertullian is also candid about where the practice comes from. Anticipating the objection that none of these customs is in Scripture, he answers: "if, for these and other such rules, you insist upon having positive Scripture injunction, you will find none. Tradition will be held forth to you as the originator of them, custom as their strengthener, and faith as their observer" (*On the Crown* 4). This is the earliest Christian defence of praying for the dead, and it rests on tradition by its author's own admission.

**Cyprian** (Carthage, about 250) shows the practice institutionalised. Writing to a church whose late member Victor had named a presbyter as his executor, contrary to a decree of the bishops, Cyprian rules that the penalty must stand: no offering is to be made for Victor's falling asleep, and no sacrifice celebrated for his repose, and his name is not to be recited in the church's prayer. The sanction only makes sense if offering the Eucharist for the dead by name was the norm.

By the middle of the third century, then, the practice is documented in Asia Minor, Rome, and North Africa: prayer for the departed by name at the Eucharist, offerings on the anniversary of death, and the petition for *refreshment* and *peace*. No text from this period speaks of purgatory, of fire, or of penalty. The dead are asked for rest.

---

## The fourth and fifth centuries: the practice everywhere

**Cyril of Jerusalem** (about 350), explaining the liturgy to the newly baptised, describes the prayer after the consecration: "Then we commemorate also those who have fallen asleep before us, first Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, Martyrs, that at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition. Then on behalf also of the Holy Fathers and Bishops who have fallen asleep before us, and in a word of all who in past years have fallen asleep among us, believing that it will be a very great benefit to the souls, for whom the supplication is put up, while that holy and most awful sacrifice is set forth" (*Catechetical Lectures* 23.9). He knows people object that a dead person cannot be helped, and answers with an analogy: if a king had banished some who had offended him, and their friends wove a crown and offered it to him on their behalf, would he not remit the punishment? So the church, offering Christ slain for our sins, "propitiates our merciful God" for the departed.

**The *Apostolic Constitutions*** (Syria, about 380) preserve a full prayer for the departed at the liturgy and prescribe memorials on the third, ninth, and thirtieth days after death and on the anniversary. The Eastern churches keep a version of this calendar to the present day.

**John Chrysostom** (Antioch and Constantinople, 386–407) preached the practice repeatedly. His argument from Scripture is Job 1:5: if Job's sons were purified by their father's sacrifice, why doubt that our offerings bring the departed some consolation? He calls the commemoration of the dead at the Eucharist an ordinance of the apostles and urges the bereaved to help their dead by prayer, offerings, and alms rather than by extravagant mourning (*Homilies on 1 Corinthians* 41; *Homilies on Philippians* 3).

**Ambrose** (Milan, about 395) prayed publicly and at length for the dead emperor Theodosius, asking God to give him "perfect rest," and said he would not cease to pray until his prayers brought the emperor to the holy mountain of the Lord. He did the same for his own brother Satyrus and for the young emperor Valentinian.

**Gregory of Nyssa** (about 380) taught, following Origen, that souls not fully purified in this life are cleansed after death by a purifying fire — the clearest fourth-century statement of the idea that later became purgatory, though Gregory's version tended toward the eventual restoration of all, which the church rejected.

**Augustine** (North Africa, 387–430) is the decisive witness for the West, and the most careful. His mother Monica, dying at Ostia, asked only one thing: "Lay this body anywhere; let not the care for that any way disquiet you: this only I request, that you would remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be" (*Confessions* 9.11, in E. B. Pusey's translation). Augustine did, and his *Confessions* end that book with a prayer for her sins and a request that everyone who reads it remember Monica and her husband Patricius at the altar. He states the principle plainly: "Nor can it be denied that the souls of the dead are benefited by the piety of their living friends, who offer the sacrifice of the Mediator, or give alms in the church on their behalf. But these services are of advantage only to those who during their lives have earned such merit, that services of this kind can help them. For there is a manner of life which is neither so good as not to require these services after death, nor so bad that such services are of no avail after death" (*Enchiridion* 110, in J. F. Shaw's translation).

Augustine's limits deserve as much notice as his affirmation. He divides the dead into three: the very good, for whom prayer is thanksgiving; the very bad, for whom it does nothing but console the living; and the middle sort, whom it helps. He grounds the practice in "the custom of the universal Church" and in 2 Maccabees, and in nothing else. He treats the purifying fire of 1 Corinthians 3 as a possibility, not a doctrine: it is not impossible, he says, that something of the kind happens after this life, and whether it does is a question that may be inquired into (*Enchiridion* 69). And in his treatise *On the Care of the Dead*, he argues that where a body is buried does the soul no good except by prompting the living to pray, and that the dead do not in general know what happens on earth — citing Isaiah 63:16, "though Abraham be ignorant of us." Everything later Catholic theology built was built on Augustine, and he built it with more hedging than his successors kept.

---

## The one dissenter

In fifteen hundred years between the apostles and the Reformation, the church records exactly one theologian who opposed prayer for the dead, and it condemned him as a heretic.

**Aerius** was a presbyter in Sebaste in Pontus around 360, an ascetic and an Arian, who broke with his bishop and gathered a following. Epiphanius of Salamis, writing his catalogue of heresies about 375, lists Aerius's errors: that a presbyter is equal to a bishop, that fixed fasts and Easter should not be observed, and that prayer and offerings for the dead are useless. Epiphanius reports his argument, which is the Reformation's argument twelve centuries early: What good can it do the dead that the living pray or give alms for them? And if the prayers of the living do help the dead, then let no one bother to be pious; let him simply arrange for friends to pray for him afterward, and he will suffer nothing.

Epiphanius's answer is instructive because it is not an appeal to Scripture. The church, he says, received the practice from the fathers, and it is useful: naming the departed at the altar confesses that they live; praying for the righteous dead distinguishes the Lord Jesus, who needs no prayer, from every human being, however holy, who does; and praying for sinners is a plea for God's mercy, which is never wrong, even where it cannot reach every sin (*Panarion* 75). Aerius's movement died with him. Its memory was kept alive chiefly by later writers who cited him to prove that rejecting prayer for the dead was an ancient heresy — which is how Melanchthon came to discuss him in 1531.

---

## From prayer to purgatory

The practice was three centuries old before anyone explained it by a place of punishment, and it took another nine to make that explanation dogma.

**Gregory the Great** (Pope, 590–604) supplied the theology. In his *Dialogues* (about 593) he taught that "for certain lesser faults" there is a purifying fire before the Last Judgment, reasoning from Matthew 12:32 that if one sin cannot be forgiven in the world to come, others can. He told the story of the monk Justus, who died in possession of three gold coins against his vow; Gregory ordered the Eucharist offered for him on thirty consecutive days, and on the thirtieth Justus appeared to his brother to say he had been received into communion. The thirty "Gregorian Masses" for a soul became a fixture of Western piety.

**All Souls' Day** (November 2) was instituted by Odilo, abbot of Cluny, about 998, for all the departed, and spread through the West.

**The word and the definition.** The Latin noun *purgatorium* as the name of a place appears in the twelfth century; the historian Jacques Le Goff argued in *The Birth of Purgatory* that the doctrine was substantially born then, a claim Catholic scholars contest by pointing to the earlier fire of Gregory and Augustine. The doctrine was first defined by a council at Lyons in 1274, in a profession of faith imposed on the Byzantine emperor: souls that die truly penitent but before making satisfaction are purged after death by purgatorial penalties, and the suffrages of the living — the Mass, prayers, alms — relieve them. The Council of Florence repeated it in 1439 in a failed union with the Greeks, over the objection of Mark of Ephesus, who accepted prayer for the dead and rejected the fire.

**Indulgences** — remissions of temporal punishment, granted by the pope from the church's treasury of merit — were first officially extended to souls in purgatory by Sixtus IV in 1476. By the early sixteenth century they were being sold, and the Dominican Johann Tetzel's campaign in Germany in 1517, with its jingle about the coin and the coffer and the soul flying out of purgatory, is what provoked Luther. Of the Ninety-Five Theses, a third concern purgatory and the dead. Thesis 27: "They preach only human doctrines who say that as soon as the money clinks into the money chest, the soul flies out of purgatory."

**Trent** answered the Reformation in its final session (December 1563) with a short decree: "there is a purgatory, and the souls there detained are helped by the suffrages of the faithful, but principally by the acceptable sacrifice of the altar." The council also ordered the bishops to suppress the "uncertain" and the "superstitious" and everything that "savours of filthy lucre." The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1992) states the modern position: "All who die in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification, so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of heaven" (1030), and the church "from the beginning" has offered prayers and above all the Eucharist for them (1032). November is kept as the month of the dead; the Requiem Mass and the prayer "Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them" remain in daily use.

**The sequence matters.** Prayer for the dead came first, as a petition for rest. Purgatory came later, as an explanation of what the prayer was for. The medieval system of Masses for money came later still, as a way of applying the explanation. The Reformers attacked the third, rejected the second, and divided over the first.

---

## The East

The Orthodox churches pray for the dead as much as Rome — arguably more, since the practice is woven through the daily and weekly cycle — and have never accepted purgatory.

The departed are commemorated at every Divine Liturgy, in the preparation of the gifts and in the great prayer after the consecration. Memorial services (*panikhida* in Slavonic, *mnemosyno* in Greek) are held on the third, ninth, and fortieth days after death, at the anniversary, and on the several Saturdays of the Souls before Lent and Pentecost, with boiled wheat (*kollyva*) blessed as a sign of the resurrection (John 12:24). The petition is for "rest with the saints," for the forgiveness of "every transgression, voluntary and involuntary," and for "a good defence before the dread judgment seat of Christ."

The theology behind it is View C above: the departed have a foretaste of their state but the Last Judgment has not happened, and God's mercy, sought by the church, remains at work. Mark of Ephesus at Florence rejected the Latin fire and the Latin idea of satisfaction while insisting on the prayers. The Confession of Dositheus (1672), adopted by a synod at Jerusalem in reaction to Calvinist influence, went further toward Rome than most Orthodox theologians now follow, affirming that souls of the penitent who died without completing satisfaction are helped by the prayers and the sacrifice of the church. Popular Orthodox teaching includes the "aerial toll-houses," trials of the soul after death, which some Orthodox theologians defend and others reject as a distortion. The whole Eastern picture is less defined than the Western, and deliberately so.

---

## The Reformation

The Reformers agreed on purgatory and disagreed on the bare prayer, and the disagreement tracks the difference between Luther and Calvin.

**Luther's path.** The Ninety-Five Theses (1517) assume purgatory and dispute only the pope's power over it. His *Sermon on Preparing to Die* (1519) still directs the dying to it. By 1528 he had moved. In the *Confession Concerning Christ's Supper* he wrote: "As for the dead, since Scripture gives us no information on the subject, I regard it as no sin to pray with free devotion in this or some similar fashion: 'Dear God, if this soul is in a condition accessible to mercy, be thou gracious to it.' And when this has been done once or twice, let it suffice." In 1530 he formally recanted purgatory, and in the Smalcald Articles (1537) called it, with all its pomp, services, and trade, a mere apparition of the devil, because it conflicts with the chief article: that Christ alone, and not the works of men, helps souls. Luther's permission of 1528 — a free prayer, once or twice, conditioned on "if" — is the historic Lutheran floor.

**The Augsburg Apology** (Melanchthon, 1531) drew the line precisely: "we know that the ancients speak of prayer for the dead, which we do not prohibit; but we disapprove of the application *ex opere operato* of the Lord's Supper on behalf of the dead" (Article 24.94). The objection was to the Mass as a work that benefits the dead by being performed, not to a Christian asking God's mercy on a departed friend. Melanchthon also took up Aerius, arguing that Epiphanius had condemned him for something other than what Rome now taught.

**Zwingli** in his Sixty-Seven Articles (1523) declared that Scripture knows nothing of purgatory, that the judgment of the departed is known to God alone, and that the less God has revealed about it the less we should try to know — while adding that he did not condemn a person who, in concern for the dead, called on God to show them mercy; what he condemned was fixing a tariff of years and lying about it for gain.

**Calvin** would have none of it. The *Institutes* (3.5.6–10) call purgatory "a deadly fiction of Satan" that makes void the cross, dismiss 2 Maccabees as non-canonical, and then face the fathers directly. He concedes that prayer for the dead was very ancient and universal, and that Augustine prayed for Monica, and answers that antiquity does not make a practice scriptural: the fathers were carried along by custom and a misplaced human tenderness, and even they, Augustine among them, admitted they had no word of God for it. Calvin's rule is that prayer must rest on the word of God, and there is none here.

**England** moved in stages. The first Book of Common Prayer (1549) kept a prayer at Communion for the departed — "We commend unto thy mercy, O Lord, all other thy servants, which are departed hence from us with the sign of faith, and now do rest in the sleep of peace" — and prayers in the burial service. The 1552 book removed them, and the Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) ruled: "The Romish Doctrine concerning Purgatory, Pardons, Worshipping and Adoration, as well of Images as of Relics, and also Invocation of Saints, is a fond thing, vainly invented, and grounded upon no warranty of Scripture, but rather repugnant to the Word of God" (Article 22). The chantries, endowed for Masses for the dead, had been dissolved in 1547. What the 1662 book kept was thanksgiving carefully shaped so that nothing is asked *for* the dead: "we also bless thy holy Name for all thy servants departed this life in thy faith and fear; beseeching thee to give us grace so to follow their good examples, that with them we may be partakers of thy heavenly kingdom." The Article's wording — "the *Romish* doctrine" — was later read by the Oxford Movement as leaving room for a non-Roman prayer for the dead, and that reading is why the practice is now found across the Anglican spectrum.

**The Westminster Confession** (1646) is the plainest Protestant statement: "Prayer is to be made for things lawful; and for all sorts of men living, or that shall live hereafter; but not for the dead, nor for those of whom it may be known that they have sinned the sin unto death" (21.4). And on the state of the dead: the righteous go immediately to heaven, the wicked to hell, and "Besides these two places, for souls separated from their bodies, the Scripture acknowledgeth none" (32.1). The Larger Catechism (question 183) repeats "but not for the dead." This is the standard for Presbyterian and most Reformed churches, and the effective position of most Baptists and evangelicals, whether or not they subscribe to it.

**What united the Reformers** was not a proof text that prayer for the dead is sinful. It was three convictions: that Scripture does not teach it; that purgatory contradicts justification by faith in a finished work; and that the traffic in Masses was a scandal. Where they differed was on whether the bare prayer, stripped of purgatory and payment, survived those convictions. Luther and Zwingli said it could, within limits. Calvin and Westminster said it could not, because prayer without a word of God to rest on is presumption.

---

## Since the Reformation

**John Wesley**, accused of popery for it in the 1740s and 1750s, replied that he prayed for the faithful departed as the primitive church had, and rejected purgatory outright, and he kept language of that kind in his own published prayers. Methodism did not follow him in this.

**Anglicanism** has moved back toward the practice. The proposed English book of 1928 and the American Book of Common Prayer of 1979 restored prayer for the departed ("Give to the departed eternal rest; let light perpetual shine upon them"), and many Anglicans pray for the dead with no doctrine of purgatory attached; evangelical Anglicans generally do not.

**C. S. Lewis** is the best-known Protestant voice on the other side. "Of course I pray for the dead," he wrote in *Letters to Malcolm* (1964). "The action is so spontaneous, so all but inevitable, that only the most compulsive theological case against it would deter me. And I hardly know how the rest of my prayers would survive if those for the dead were forbidden." He also said he believed in purgatory — not the "Romish doctrine" of the Articles, which he rejected, but a cleansing that the soul itself would ask for.

**The Latter-day Saints** practise baptism for the dead, by proxy in their temples, on the basis of 1 Corinthians 15:29, and understand it as extending salvation to those who died without the gospel. No other body does this, and it is the one modern practice that Luke 16:26 and Hebrews 9:27 most directly contradict.

**Lutheran and Reformed churches today** hold their confessional positions. Funeral liturgies across Protestantism nonetheless nearly all contain a *commendation* — "into your hands, O merciful Saviour, we commend your servant" — which entrusts the dead to God without asking anything for them, and which is the point where Protestant practice comes closest to the prayer it declines.

---

## Judaism

The practice 2 Maccabees records has continued in Judaism to the present, and a Christian reader should know its shape, because it is the setting the New Testament was written in.

The **Mourner's Kaddish** is recited by the bereaved for eleven months after a parent's death and on the anniversary (*yahrzeit*). Its text says nothing about death; it is a doxology, magnifying and sanctifying God's name. The belief that reciting it benefits the soul of the departed is medieval, resting on a story that Rabbi Akiva taught a dead man's son to lead the congregation in praise and so released his father from punishment. The eleven months reflect a rabbinic teaching that the wicked are judged in Gehinnom for twelve; to say Kaddish for the full twelve would imply the parent was wicked. **Yizkor** ("May he remember"), a memorial prayer said four times a year in Ashkenazi synagogues, includes a pledge of charity on the departed's behalf, and the prayer **El Malei Rachamim** ("God, full of mercy") asks rest for the soul "under the wings of the Divine Presence." The rabbis found a scriptural basis in Deuteronomy 21:8, reading "whom thou hast redeemed" as those already dead, and concluding that the dead need atonement and the living can seek it for them.

None of this is Christian teaching, and Judaism has no purgatory in the Latin sense. But it shows that the instinct to pray for the dead is at least as old as the Maccabees, that it was alive in the synagogues the apostles preached in, and that it survived there without any of the machinery the Reformation objected to.

# Part III — What a Christian Can Do

## Start with what is certain

Whatever else is disputed, the following are not, and they are enough to pray on.

- **The dead in Christ are with Christ.** "To day shalt thou be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43); "absent from the body, and … present with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8); "to depart, and to be with Christ; which is far better" (Philippians 1:23).
- **They are at rest.** "Blessed are the dead which die in the Lord … that they may rest from their labours" (Revelation 14:13).
- **Nothing separates them from God's love.** "Neither death, nor life … shall be able to separate us from the love of God" (Romans 8:38–39).
- **They are waiting, as we are, for the resurrection.** "The dead in Christ shall rise first" (1 Thessalonians 4:16); "that they without us should not be made perfect" (Hebrews 11:40).
- **God's judgment is right.** "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25).
- **The secret things are his.** "The secret things belong unto the LORD our God" (Deuteronomy 29:29).
- **You are to comfort one another.** "Wherefore comfort one another with these words" (1 Thessalonians 4:18).

---

## Grief is not doubt

> John 11:35 — Jesus wept.

He wept at a grave he was about to empty. Grief is not a failure of faith and does not need to be prayed away; Paul's word is not "sorrow not" but "sorrow not, even as others which have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). The Old Testament's thirty days for Moses, the seven for Jacob, the lamentation over Stephen, and "weep with them that weep" (Romans 12:15) are all Scripture's permission to mourn. Ecclesiastes goes further and calls the house of mourning better than the house of feasting, because "the living will lay it to his heart" (Ecclesiastes 7:2). Much of the pressure to pray for the dead is grief looking for somewhere to go. It is allowed to go into weeping, and into the prayers below.

---

## The prayers every Christian can pray

Each of these is modelled or commanded in Scripture, and no Christian tradition objects to any of them. Together they cover most of what a grieving person actually wants to say.

**1. Thanksgiving for the person.** "I thank my God upon every remembrance of you" (Philippians 1:3). The 1662 Prayer Book's "we bless thy holy Name for all thy servants departed this life in thy faith and fear" is this prayer, and the strictest Puritan could pray it. Name the person, thank God for what they were, and for what God did through them.

**2. Commendation.** Entrusting the dead to God. This is the prayer Jesus prayed for himself — "Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit" (Luke 23:46) — and Stephen: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59). It is Psalm 31:5, "Into thine hand I commit my spirit," and Paul's "I know whom I have believed, and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day" (2 Timothy 1:12). Commending someone to God does not inform him of anything or ask him to alter anything. It says: *they are yours; I hand them to you.* Nearly every Protestant funeral rite includes it, and it is the prayer to reach for when you do not know what else to say.

**3. "Thy kingdom come."** The dead in Christ are waiting for the resurrection and the kingdom (Revelation 6:10–11; 1 Thessalonians 4:16). When you pray "Thy kingdom come" (Matthew 6:10) or "Even so, come, Lord Jesus" (Revelation 22:20), you are asking for the one thing they have not yet received. This is the nearest Scripture comes to modelling a prayer for the dead, and it is in the Lord's Prayer.

**4. Prayer for the living who grieve.** The only bereavement instruction the New Testament gives is directed at the survivors. Pray for them, by name, and keep praying after the funeral, when the casseroles stop.

**5. Prayer about your own death.** "So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom" (Psalms 90:12). A death nearby is Scripture's own occasion for this prayer; Sirach's advice to the mourner is "Remember my judgment: for thine also shall be so; yesterday for me, and to day for thee" (Sirach 38:22 *(Apocrypha)*).

**6. Prayer of trust in the judgment.** You may not know where a person stood with God. You may pray, as Abraham did, that the Judge of all the earth will do right — and mean it as trust rather than as a demand. This is not a prayer for the dead. It is a prayer about God, and it is the one prayer that fits every case.

---

## If your church prays for the dead

Catholic and Orthodox Christians are asked by their churches to pray for the departed, at the liturgy and privately, and this document is not the place to argue them out of it. Three things from the material above are worth holding onto.

**Know what your church does and does not claim.** Neither Rome nor the East teaches that prayer converts the damned, that the dead are saved by the works of the living, or that a soul's destiny is decided after death. Prayer for the dead in both traditions is for those who died *in Christ*, and asks God's mercy in completing what he began. Augustine's caution — that prayer for the very good is thanksgiving, and for the very bad, consolation to the living — is still the church's teaching, and it guards against the idea that any amount of prayer can do what only grace does.

**Keep it prayer.** The abuse that split the Western church was not prayer for the dead but its conversion into a transaction: so many Masses for so much money for so many years. Trent itself condemned that. Prayer for the departed that is not asked of God as a free mercy is not the thing the fathers did.

**Let the Gospel be the ground.** The reason to hope for a departed Christian is not the number of prayers offered but Christ's finished work, which every tradition confesses (Hebrews 10:14). Prayer for the dead, where it is practised, is a response to that hope, not a supplement to it.

---

## If your church does not

Most Protestants belong to churches that do not pray for the dead, and many have never been told why beyond "that's Catholic." The reasons are better than that, and knowing them makes the position easier to hold with peace rather than defensiveness.

**The actual reasons.** First, Scripture nowhere teaches the practice, and the Reformers held that prayer must rest on God's word, not on custom, however old (Calvin's point, and Tertullian's own admission). Second, the dead in Christ are already "made perfect" (Hebrews 12:23), already past condemnation (John 5:24), already with the Lord (2 Corinthians 5:8); there is nothing left for prayer to obtain for them, and a prayer that assumes otherwise implies that Christ's one offering has not "perfected for ever them that are sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14). Third, the dead outside Christ are past a fixed gulf (Luke 16:26), and prayer cannot cross it. The Reformed refusal is not coldness. It is the conclusion that the dead in Christ *need nothing from us* — which, rightly heard, is the comfort, not the loss.

**What your confession leaves open.** Everything in the list above: thanksgiving, commendation, "Thy kingdom come," prayer for the bereaved, prayer about your own death, and trust in the Judge. None of it is prayer *for* the dead in the disputed sense, and all of it is Scripture.

**The historic Protestant floor.** If you find yourself unable not to pray for someone, know that Luther, in 1528, allowed exactly that — a free prayer, "Dear God, if this soul is in a condition accessible to mercy, be thou gracious to it," once or twice, and then let it suffice — and that Zwingli declined to condemn a prayer of solicitude for the dead, and that the Augsburg Apology says the Lutheran churches "do not prohibit" it. That is not the Reformed position, and Westminster forbids it. But it is a Protestant position with a long pedigree, and it is a long way from purgatory.

**The other end.** C. S. Lewis prayed for the dead as a matter of course and said so. He is not a confession and did not claim to be. He is evidence that a serious Protestant Christian can arrive there honestly.

---

## When the urge comes anyway

Almost everyone who has buried someone they love has found a prayer for them forming before any theology could intervene. Three questions sort those prayers.

**1. Does it ask God for something he has said he will not do?** A prayer that the damned be released, or that a destiny be reversed, asks against Luke 16:26 and Hebrews 9:27. No Christian tradition prays it. If the prayer forming in you is that, redirect it into trust: *the Judge of all the earth will do right.*

**2. Does it turn toward the dead person instead of toward God?** Talking to the dead, asking them for signs, seeking contact, is the thing Deuteronomy 18 and Isaiah 8:19 forbid, and grief makes it tempting. Prayer goes to God. If you want to say something to the person, say it to God about them.

**3. Does it become a transaction, or a substitute?** If prayer for the dead starts to feel like a debt you are paying off, or a way of doing something for the person when you could have done more for them alive, it has become what the Reformation rightly attacked. And if it takes the place of what you owe the living — the other mourners, the ones still to be reached with the gospel — then Abraham's word to the rich man applies: *they have Moses and the prophets*. The living are the ones who can still hear.

A prayer that passes all three — "Lord, I thank you for her, I commend her to you, and I ask you to be merciful to her as you have promised" — is, on the strictest reading, thanksgiving and commendation and a confession of the gospel, and on the broadest reading, everything the early church asked. It may be that the two readings are closer than the confessions make them look.

---

## The hardest case

When the person who died gave no sign of faith.

Scripture offers no promise of conversion after death, and this document will not invent one; the whole weight of Luke 16, Hebrews 9:27, and "now is the day of salvation" is against it, and every tradition treated here agrees. But Scripture also offers no one the knowledge of another person's last hour. The thief on the cross was saved in the time it took to say a sentence, with no one watching but Jesus. "The secret things belong unto the LORD."

What you can do is refuse two errors. Do not construct a doctrine to soothe yourself; it will not hold, and you will know it. And do not torment yourself with a verdict you were never given the authority to pronounce; that is not humility but a claim to know what only God knows. Between those two lies the only honest prayer: that the Judge of all the earth will do right, that his mercy is wider than your sight, and that you will trust him with what you cannot see. Then turn to the living, for whom something can still be done.

---

## What not to do

- **Do not consult the dead** — mediums, séances, "signs," or messages. Deuteronomy 18:10–12; Isaiah 8:19; 1 Samuel 28.
- **Do not pay for prayer, or treat prayer as payment.** Psalm 49:7. The traffic in Masses is the one thing on this subject that every tradition now repudiates.
- **Do not pray as though informing God of the person's merits.** He knows. Commend, do not plead a case.
- **Do not let concern for the dead displace the gospel for the living.** John 9:4; 2 Corinthians 6:2; Luke 16:29.
- **Do not mourn without hope, or forbid yourself to mourn.** 1 Thessalonians 4:13; John 11:35.

---

## The one thing everyone agrees on

Every tradition, every confession, and every text in Part I converges on one point: the living have today.

> Ecclesiastes 9:10 — Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest.

> Hebrews 3:13 — But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin.

The Catholic who prays for the souls in purgatory and the Presbyterian who will not both believe that the decisive time is now, that the word of God is the means, and that the people who most need prayer are the ones who can still hear it. Whatever you conclude about the dead, that is the counsel Abraham gave the rich man, and it is the one thing this whole subject, rightly handled, should send you back to.

## B. 2 Maccabees 12:38–45 in full

From the 1611 King James Apocrypha, as transcribed in the CrossWire OSIS edition (see [How to Use This Document](#the-source-of-the-quotations)). Not in [`kjv/`](kjv).

> 38 So Judas gathered his host, and came into the city of Odollam, And when the seventh day came, they purified themselves, as the custom was, and kept the sabbath in the same place.
>
> 39 And upon the day following, as the use had been, Judas and his company came to take up the bodies of them that were slain, and to bury them with their kinsmen in their fathers' graves.
>
> 40 Now under the coats of every one that was slain they found things consecrated to the idols of the Jamnites, which is forbidden the Jews by the law. Then every man saw that this was the cause wherefore they were slain.
>
> 41 All men therefore praising the Lord, the righteous Judge, who had opened the things that were hid,
>
> 42 Betook themselves unto prayer, and besought him that the sin committed might wholly be put out of remembrance. Besides, that noble Judas exhorted the people to keep themselves from sin, forsomuch as they saw before their eyes the things that came to pass for the sins of those that were slain.
>
> 43 And when he had made a gathering throughout the company to the sum of two thousand drachms of silver, he sent it to Jerusalem to offer a sin offering, doing therein very well and honestly, in that he was mindful of the resurrection:
>
> 44 For if he had not hoped that they that were slain should have risen again, it had been superfluous and vain to pray for the dead.
>
> 45 And also in that he perceived that there was great favour laid up for those that died godly, it was an holy and good thought. Whereupon he made a reconciliation for the dead, that they might be delivered from sin.

Verse numbering follows the KJV; some modern editions (and the Vulgate) number verses 43–45 differently, and the Latin text of verse 45 ("it is therefore a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead, that they may be loosed from sins") is the form most often quoted in Catholic sources.

## E. Honest caveats

**The biblical material is thin and the literature is not.** One Apocryphal passage, one ambiguous prayer for Onesiphorus, one unexplained rite at Corinth, and a great deal of silence. Everything else on either side is inference from passages about something else — the state of the dead, the finality of judgment, the scope of prayer. Books that present Scripture as plainly settling the question in either direction are presenting more than the text supplies.

**The argument from silence is used by both sides, and it is weak for both.** This document has tried to say each time it is used that it is being used.

**"Praying for the dead" names two different things.** The bare prayer for mercy and the system of purgatory, indulgences, and Masses have different histories, different defenders, and different objectors. A reader who has rejected the second has not yet decided about the first, and a reader who accepts the first has not thereby accepted the second. Much polemic on this subject, in both directions, gains its force by refusing to distinguish them.

**The historical sketch is a sketch.** The fathers are quoted in standard nineteenth-century translations, from memory of those translations, and the reader who wants to build on a quotation should check it in a printed edition. Dates for the earliest witnesses are approximate. The account of Orthodox theology flattens real diversity, and the account of Judaism is limited to what bears on the Christian question.

**On the state of the dead, this document takes View A as the best reading of the New Testament,** for the reasons given under "Texts cited against" — chiefly Luke 23:43, Philippians 1:23, and Hebrews 10:14 — and it takes the view that Scripture nowhere teaches prayer for the dead in the disputed sense and nowhere expressly forbids the bare prayer. Readers who weigh 2 Maccabees, the fathers, or Hebrews 11:40 more heavily will land elsewhere, and their texts are quoted here so that they can.

**Nothing here is pastoral counsel for a particular person.** Grief is not a doctrinal problem, and someone in the middle of it needs a pastor, a friend, and time more than an argument. Part III is written to be usable by such a person, but it is not a substitute for those things.

**Beyond Scripture,** the short primary sources are the best. Augustine's *Enchiridion* chapters 109–110 and *Confessions* book 9 show the practice at its most thoughtful; Tertullian's *On the Crown* 3–4 shows it at its earliest, with its author's admission of where it comes from; Epiphanius, *Panarion* 75, preserves the one ancient objection. On the Protestant side, Calvin's *Institutes* 3.5.6–10 is the classic case against, and the Augsburg Apology, Article 24, the classic case for tolerating the bare prayer. C. S. Lewis's *Letters to Malcolm*, chapter 20, is two pages and says what a modern Protestant who prays for the dead thinks he is doing. All are freely available.
