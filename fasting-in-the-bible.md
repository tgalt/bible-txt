# Fasting
## What Scripture Says, How It Was Practised, and Where It Belongs in a Christian Life

---

## How to Use This Document

This document answers three questions, in order:

1. **What does the Bible actually say about fasting?** — every passage, quoted, sorted, and read in context (Part I)
2. **How are you supposed to do it?** — what Scripture prescribes, what it leaves open, and what that looks like in practice (Part II)
3. **How does it fit into a Christian life?** — where fasting sits alongside grace, prayer, the body, and Christian freedom (Part III)

Part I is reference material. Parts II and III are the practical payoff. If you only want to know how to begin, Part II stands on its own — but the reason Part I comes first is that most bad fasting comes from good intentions attached to a wrong idea of what fasting is *for*, and the Bible is unusually blunt about that.

### The source of the quotations

Scripture is quoted from the **King James Version (1769)**, the text carried in [`kjv/`](kjv) in this repository. Every quotation here was taken directly from that file, so any verse can be checked at its source:

```sh
grep '^Isaiah 58:6 ' kjv/kjv.txt
```

The KJV is used because this document quotes heavily, and the KJV is in the public domain. Where its English has drifted far enough from modern usage to mislead, the modern sense is given in brackets or in the surrounding comment — *meat* means food of any kind, not flesh; *an hungred* means hungry; *bottles* are wineskins; *closet* is an inner room.

### A note on what this document is not

This is a study of the biblical material, not a ruling from a church. On the questions where sincere Christians have disagreed for centuries — whether fasting is commanded or commended, whether it should follow a calendar, what it accomplishes — the disagreement is laid out rather than settled, and the passages that drive each side are quoted so you can weigh them. Where this document does take a position, it says so and shows its work.

**On health:** fasting from food is a physical act with physical consequences. See [A Necessary Caution](#a-necessary-caution) in Part II before beginning any fast of significant length. Some people should not fast from food at all, and Scripture gives no one a reason to harm themselves.

# Part I — What the Bible Says

## The words themselves

The Hebrew verb is **צוּם** (*tsum*), "to fast," with its noun **צוֹם** (*tsom*), "a fast." The Greek is **νηστεύω** (*nēsteuō*) and **νηστεία** (*nēsteia*), from a root meaning "not eating." In both languages the plain sense is the same and it is narrow: *to go without food*. Not without television, not without spending — without food.

That plain sense matters, because "fasting" in modern speech has stretched to cover any voluntary abstinence. The stretch is not illegitimate as an application, and Part III takes it seriously. But it is an application. When Scripture says *fast*, it means the stomach.

Three details of vocabulary are worth carrying into everything that follows.

**First, the Law never uses the word for its own commanded fast.** The one day of abstinence God required of Israel is prescribed with a different phrase: **עִנָּה נֶפֶשׁ** (*innah nephesh*), "to afflict the soul." This is broader than skipping meals — it means self-humbling, of which going without food is the visible part.

**Second, Scripture sometimes uses the word for involuntary hunger.** When Jesus says he will not send the crowd away *fasting*, he means hungry, not devout:

> **Matthew 15:32** — Then Jesus called his disciples unto him, and said, I have compassion on the multitude, because they continue with me now three days, and have nothing to eat: and I will not send them away fasting, lest they faint in the way.

The same is true of the sailors in the storm who had "continued fasting" for fourteen days (Acts 27:33) — that is shipwreck, not devotion. Any list of "fasting verses" that counts these as teaching on the spiritual discipline is padding its numbers.

**Third, "the fast" can be a calendar marker.** In Acts 27:9 Luke dates the voyage by it:

> **Acts 27:9** — Now when much time was spent, and when sailing was now dangerous, because the fast was now already past, Paul admonished them,

"The fast" here is the Day of Atonement — used the way we would say "after Michaelmas." It tells us the season was late, roughly October, and sailing had turned dangerous.

Counting only the word-family, fasting appears in **65 verses**: 38 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New. Subtract the involuntary and calendrical uses and you are left with roughly sixty verses of actual teaching and narrative — a real but modest body of material, scattered across seventeen centuries of biblical history. Appendix A lists every one.

## The one fast God commanded

Under the Law of Moses, God commanded exactly **one** fast day in the year: the **Day of Atonement**, the tenth day of the seventh month.

> **Leviticus 16:29** — And this shall be a statute for ever unto you: that in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, ye shall afflict your souls, and do no work at all, whether it be one of your own country, or a stranger that sojourneth among you:

> **Leviticus 16:31** — It shall be a sabbath of rest unto you, and ye shall afflict your souls, by a statute for ever.

> **Leviticus 23:27** — Also on the tenth day of this seventh month there shall be a day of atonement: it shall be an holy convocation unto you; and ye shall afflict your souls, and offer an offering made by fire unto the LORD.

The command was serious. Refusing it carried the heaviest sanction in the covenant:

> **Leviticus 23:29** — For whatsoever soul it be that shall not be afflicted in that same day, he shall be cut off from among his people.

It is repeated a third time in Numbers 29:7, and Leviticus 23:32 fixes its hours — "from even unto even," sunset to sunset, which is why Jewish fasts run evening to evening to this day.

Three observations follow, and they shape the whole biblical picture.

**One commanded fast, and it is tied to atonement.** The single day Israel was required to go without food was the day the nation's sin was dealt with — the one day the high priest entered the Holy of Holies with blood. Fasting, at its origin in the Law, is the bodily posture of a people who know they need forgiveness they cannot manufacture.

**Everything else is voluntary.** Every other fast in the Bible — and there are many — was chosen by someone, not required by God. This is the structural fact most often missed. The biblical norm for fasting is not *obligation* but *response*: something happens, and people fast.

**The commanded fast has been fulfilled.** The New Testament's argument in Hebrews is that Christ has entered the true Holy of Holies once for all. The annual day of atonement was a shadow; the body is Christ (Colossians 2:17). Which means the *one* fast Scripture ever commanded is the one fast a Christian is explicitly not bound to keep — a point Part III returns to.

## The fasts Israel added

After the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, the exiles took up four annual fasts of their own, marking stages of the catastrophe. Zechariah names them:

> **Zechariah 8:19** — Thus saith the LORD of hosts; The fast of the fourth month, and the fast of the fifth, and the fast of the seventh, and the fast of the tenth, shall be to the house of Judah joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts; therefore love the truth and peace.

The fourth month marked the breaching of the city walls; the fifth, the burning of the temple; the seventh, the murder of Gedaliah; the tenth, the beginning of the siege. None of them was commanded. Israel instituted them, and kept them for seventy years.

Then a delegation came to ask the prophet whether they should keep going. God's answer is one of the most searching things said about fasting anywhere in Scripture:

> **Zechariah 7:5** — Speak unto all the people of the land, and to the priests, saying, When ye fasted and mourned in the fifth and seventh month, even those seventy years, did ye at all fast unto me, even to me?

> **Zechariah 7:6** — And when ye did eat, and when ye did drink, did not ye eat for yourselves, and drink for yourselves?

Seventy years of faithfully-kept fasts, and the question God asks is whether a single one of them was actually *for him*. The implied answer is no. They had fasted the way they ate — for themselves, out of habit and national grief, with God as the occasion rather than the object.

And what does he ask for instead?

> **Zechariah 7:9** — Thus speaketh the LORD of hosts, saying, Execute true judgment, and shew mercy and compassions every man to his brother:

> **Zechariah 7:10** — And oppress not the widow, nor the fatherless, the stranger, nor the poor; and let none of you imagine evil against his brother in your heart.

This pattern — a question about fasting answered with a command about justice — is not a one-off. It happens again in Isaiah 58, and it is the single most consistent note the Bible sounds on this subject.

The promise attached is worth noticing too. Zechariah 8:19 does not abolish the four fasts; it announces they will become *feasts*. The days of mourning are not deleted, they are transposed. Grief is not the last word over any date in the calendar.

## Why people fasted: six occasions

Strip out the commanded fast and the involuntary hunger, and the remaining biblical fasts fall into six overlapping occasions. Almost every fast in Scripture is one or more of these.

### 1. Grief

The oldest and most instinctive use. Sorrow takes away appetite, and the fast is simply the honest expression of that.

> **1 Samuel 31:13** — And they took their bones, and buried them under a tree at Jabesh, and fasted seven days.

> **2 Samuel 1:12** — And they mourned, and wept, and fasted until even, for Saul, and for Jonathan his son, and for the people of the LORD, and for the house of Israel; because they were fallen by the sword.

Note that these are not petitions. Saul is dead; nothing is being asked for. The fast is grief with a body.

### 2. Repentance

Fasting as the physical shape of "we have sinned." Israel at Mizpeh:

> **1 Samuel 7:6** — And they gathered together to Mizpeh, and drew water, and poured it out before the LORD, and fasted on that day, and said there, We have sinned against the LORD. And Samuel judged the children of Israel in Mizpeh.

Nineveh, on hearing Jonah:

> **Jonah 3:5** — So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them.

> **Jonah 3:7** — And he caused it to be proclaimed and published through Nineveh by the decree of the king and his nobles, saying, Let neither man nor beast, herd nor flock, taste any thing: let them not feed, nor drink water:

> **Jonah 3:10** — And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not.

Verse 10 repays attention. God saw their works — **that they turned from their evil way**. Not that they skipped meals. The fast was real and God responded, but what the text says he *saw* was the turning.

Joel presses the same point into a command:

> **Joel 2:12** — Therefore also now, saith the LORD, turn ye even to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning:

> **Joel 2:13** — And rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the LORD your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil.

*Rend your heart, and not your garments.* Joel does not cancel the outward act — verse 15 goes straight on to "sanctify a fast" — but he insists the outward act is worthless as a substitute for the inward one. Both verses are in the same paragraph, and keeping them together is the whole art of fasting.

Even a thoroughly wicked man's fast gets a response. When Ahab, of all people, humbles himself:

> **1 Kings 21:27** — And it came to pass, when Ahab heard those words, that he rent his clothes, and put sackcloth upon his flesh, and fasted, and lay in sackcloth, and went softly.

God tells Elijah he will delay the promised judgment. Whatever else fasting is, God is not indifferent to it.

### 3. Crisis and danger

When the situation is beyond managing, people in Scripture stop eating and start asking. Jehoshaphat, facing invasion:

> **2 Chronicles 20:3** — And Jehoshaphat feared, and set himself to seek the LORD, and proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah.

Ezra, about to cross hostile country with the temple treasure and no escort, having told the king that God would protect them:

> **Ezra 8:21** — Then I proclaimed a fast there, at the river of Ahava, that we might afflict ourselves before our God, to seek of him a right way for us, and for our little ones, and for all our substance.

> **Ezra 8:23** — So we fasted and besought our God for this: and he was intreated of us.

Esther, before risking her life uninvited in the king's presence:

> **Esther 4:16** — Go, gather together all the Jews that are present in Shushan, and fast ye for me, and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day: I also and my maidens will fast likewise; and so will I go in unto the king, which is not according to the law: and if I perish, I perish.

Esther's is the most remarkable of these, because the book of Esther never once mentions God — and the only religious act in it is a fast.

### 4. Intercession for another

David, when the child born to Bathsheba fell ill:

> **2 Samuel 12:16** — David therefore besought God for the child; and David fasted, and went in, and lay all night upon the earth.

The sequel is one of the most theologically important passages on fasting in the Bible, because it shows a fast that did not get what it asked for — and shows the man who prayed it accepting that without bitterness:

> **2 Samuel 12:22** — And he said, While the child was yet alive, I fasted and wept: for I said, Who can tell whether GOD will be gracious to me, that the child may live?

> **2 Samuel 12:23** — But now he is dead, wherefore should I fast? can I bring him back again? I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me.

*Who can tell.* David's fasting was a plea, not a lever. When the answer came back no, he washed, ate, and worshipped. Anyone who fasts should memorise verse 22.

The Psalms show the same intercessory instinct, and note who it is offered for:

> **Psalms 35:13** — But as for me, when they were sick, my clothing was sackcloth: I humbled my soul with fasting; and my prayer returned into mine own bosom.

The people David fasted for in Psalm 35 are, in context, his enemies.

### 5. Seeking guidance and revelation

Daniel, having worked out from Jeremiah that the seventy years were nearly up:

> **Daniel 9:3** — And I set my face unto the Lord God, to seek by prayer and supplications, with fasting, and sackcloth, and ashes:

Daniel again, in a partial fast of three weeks that precedes the vision of chapters 10–12:

> **Daniel 10:2** — In those days I Daniel was mourning three full weeks.

> **Daniel 10:3** — I ate no pleasant bread, neither came flesh nor wine in my mouth, neither did I anoint myself at all, till three whole weeks were fulfilled.

The judges' war council at Bethel (Judges 20:26), Nehemiah on hearing of Jerusalem's ruin (Nehemiah 1:4), and the assembly of Nehemiah 9:1 all fast while seeking direction.

### 6. Consecration and commissioning

The New Testament's distinctive contribution. Twice in Acts, fasting accompanies the sending of people into ministry:

> **Acts 13:2** — As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them.

> **Acts 13:3** — And when they had fasted and prayed, and laid their hands on them, they sent them away.

> **Acts 14:23** — And when they had ordained them elders in every church, and had prayed with fasting, they commended them to the Lord, on whom they believed.

Acts 13:2 deserves a careful look, because it is frequently overread. The church at Antioch was worshipping and fasting when the Spirit spoke. The text does not say the fasting produced the message, and it does not present the fast as a technique for extracting guidance. What it shows is a church attentive and unhurried — and God speaking into that attention. The order in 13:3 is also worth noting: they had *already* heard, and they fasted again before sending. Fasting frames the decision on both sides.

## The Bible's longest teaching on fasting is a rebuke

If you read only one chapter on fasting, read **Isaiah 58**. It is the most sustained treatment in Scripture, and it is an indictment.

The setting is a people who are, on paper, doing everything right:

> **Isaiah 58:2** — Yet they seek me daily, and delight to know my ways, as a nation that did righteousness, and forsook not the ordinance of their God: they ask of me the ordinances of justice; they take delight in approaching to God.

They are also, on paper, puzzled. They have complied, and nothing has happened:

> **Isaiah 58:3** — Wherefore have we fasted, say they, and thou seest not? wherefore have we afflicted our soul, and thou takest no knowledge? Behold, in the day of your fast ye find pleasure, and exact all your labours.

The complaint is a transaction that failed to clear: *we paid, why no delivery?* God's reply names three faults, and they are worth separating.

**The fast changed nothing about how they treated people.** Mid-verse, God answers his own question: in the day of your fast you pursue your own pleasure and drive your workers. The fast was a religious performance running in parallel with ordinary exploitation, touching it nowhere.

**The fast made them worse company, not better.**

> **Isaiah 58:4** — Behold, ye fast for strife and debate, and to smite with the fist of wickedness: ye shall not fast as ye do this day, to make your voice to be heard on high.

Hungry people are irritable, and these had let the irritation run. A fast that ends in a fight has, by that fact, failed. This is a startlingly practical test and it survives translation to any century: *if fasting makes you harder to live with, something has gone wrong in it.*

**The fast was theatre.**

> **Isaiah 58:5** — Is it such a fast that I have chosen? a day for a man to afflict his soul? is it to bow down his head as a bulrush, and to spread sackcloth and ashes under him? wilt thou call this a fast, and an acceptable day to the LORD?

A bulrush bends its head because a bulrush is a bulrush. The posture is real and means nothing.

Then comes the redefinition, and it is not what a reader expects. Asked what fast God has chosen, God does not describe a better technique of abstaining. He describes justice and generosity:

> **Isaiah 58:6** — Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke?

> **Isaiah 58:7** — Is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house? when thou seest the naked, that thou cover him; and that thou hide not thyself from thine own flesh?

Notice the exchange at the centre of verse 7. In an ordinary fast, your bread goes uneaten. In the fast God chooses, your bread goes **to the hungry**. The same loaf, a different destination — and that difference is the difference between the fast God rejects and the fast he accepts. The bread you do not eat is meant to end up somewhere.

And then the promises, which are lavish:

> **Isaiah 58:8** — Then shall thy light break forth as the morning, and thine health shall spring forth speedily: and thy righteousness shall go before thee; the glory of the LORD shall be thy rereward.

> **Isaiah 58:9** — Then shalt thou call, and the LORD shall answer; thou shalt cry, and he shall say, Here I am. If thou take away from the midst of thee the yoke, the putting forth of the finger, and speaking vanity;

> **Isaiah 58:11** — And the LORD shall guide thee continually, and satisfy thy soul in drought, and make fat thy bones: and thou shalt be like a watered garden, and like a spring of water, whose waters fail not.

The very things they had fasted to obtain — God's attention, God's answer, God's guidance — are promised freely, on the other side of repentance in how they treat the vulnerable.

A caution about how this chapter is used. It is sometimes read as *abolishing* fasting: God doesn't want your empty stomach, he wants social justice, so stop fasting and go volunteer. That reading goes further than the text. Isaiah is not told to stop fasting; he is told what a fast is supposed to be attached to. The same prophetic tradition that produced Isaiah 58 produced Joel 2:15, "sanctify a fast" — and Jesus, who knew both, assumed his disciples would fast. The chapter kills fasting as a self-contained religious transaction. It does not kill fasting.

The counterpart in Jeremiah shows how far the rot can go:

> **Jeremiah 14:12** — When they fast, I will not hear their cry; and when they offer burnt offering and an oblation, I will not accept them: but I will consume them by the sword, and by the famine, and by the pestilence.

And the most damning example in Scripture is Jezebel's. To arrange a judicial murder and steal a man's vineyard, she needs a solemn assembly for the false accusation, so she orders one:

> **1 Kings 21:9** — And she wrote in the letters, saying, Proclaim a fast, and set Naboth on high among the people:

A fast proclaimed as the cover for judicial murder. It is the exact inversion of Isaiah 58:6 — the religious form of "loose the bands of wickedness" used to tighten them. Whatever power fasting has, it has none at all to sanctify what it decorates.

## Jesus and fasting

### He fasted

> **Matthew 4:2** — And when he had fasted forty days and forty nights, he was afterward an hungred.

> **Luke 4:1** — And Jesus being full of the Holy Ghost returned from Jordan, and was led by the Spirit into the wilderness,

> **Luke 4:2** — Being forty days tempted of the devil. And in those days he did eat nothing: and when they were ended, he afterward hungered.

The placement is deliberate. The fast comes immediately after the baptism, where the Father has just said "This is my beloved Son" — and immediately before a temptation whose opening move is "If thou be the Son of God, command that these stones be made bread." Jesus fasts at the start of his ministry, not as preparation for a religious career but as the setting in which his identity is tested. And the first temptation is precisely to end the fast on his own authority.

His answer — *man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God* — is quoted from Deuteronomy 8:3, where it explains why God let Israel go hungry in the wilderness. That sentence is as close as Scripture comes to a one-line theology of fasting.

### He assumed his followers would fast

The Sermon on the Mount treats fasting as one of three ordinary practices of the religious life, alongside giving and prayer, and gives each the same structure: *don't do it to be seen; do it in secret; your Father will reward you.*

> **Matthew 6:16** — Moreover when ye fast, be not, as the hypocrites, of a sad countenance: for they disfigure their faces, that they may appear unto men to fast. Verily I say unto you, They have their reward.

> **Matthew 6:17** — But thou, when thou fastest, anoint thine head, and wash thy face;

> **Matthew 6:18** — That thou appear not unto men to fast, but unto thy Father which is in secret: and thy Father, which seeth in secret, shall reward thee openly.

Two things are load-bearing here.

**"When," not "if."** Jesus says *when ye fast* in exactly the way he says *when thou doest thine alms* (6:2) and *when thou prayest* (6:5). No one reads those as optional. This is the strongest argument that fasting is normal Christian practice rather than a specialist's option — though note what it is not: it is an assumption, not a command, and it fixes no frequency. Jesus never says how often.

**"They have their reward"** is an accountant's phrase. The Greek *apechō* is the word used on receipts: *paid in full*. The hypocrites wanted to be seen fasting, and they were seen. Transaction complete, nothing further owing. The warning is not that God punishes showy fasting but that showy fasting gets exactly what it was for and nothing more.

The remedy — *anoint thine head, and wash thy face* — is not a special holiness ritual. Oil and washing were ordinary grooming, what any man did on any normal morning. Jesus' instruction is: **look normal.** Do not signal. If anyone can tell you are fasting, you are doing it wrong.

That instruction quietly rules out a great deal. Announcing a fast, posting about it, dropping it into conversation, adopting a pained manner at lunchtime — all of it falls under "appear unto men to fast."

Two qualifications keep this from becoming a new legalism. First, Jesus is addressing *individual, voluntary* fasting; the corporate fasts of Scripture (Joel 2:15, Acts 13:2) are announced by necessity, because a congregation cannot fast together in secret from each other. Secrecy binds the motive, not the logistics. Second, the same sermon says "let your light so shine before men" (Matthew 5:16). The difference is the audience you are performing for, not whether anyone ever finds out.

### He explained why his disciples did not fast

The one time Jesus is questioned about fasting directly, his answer is the hinge of the whole subject:

> **Matthew 9:14** — Then came to him the disciples of John, saying, Why do we and the Pharisees fast oft, but thy disciples fast not?

> **Matthew 9:15** — And Jesus said unto them, Can the children of the bridechamber mourn, as long as the bridegroom is with them? but the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken from them, and then shall they fast.

Mark's version is sharper still — not merely that they *do not*, but that they *cannot*:

> **Mark 2:19** — And Jesus said unto them, Can the children of the bridechamber fast, while the bridegroom is with them? as long as they have the bridegroom with them, they cannot fast.

> **Mark 2:20** — But the days will come, when the bridegroom shall be taken away from them, and then shall they fast in those days.

The image is a wedding. Fasting at a wedding is not wicked, it is *absurd* — a category error, like wearing black to the reception. While the bridegroom is present, feasting is the only fitting response.

This yields three conclusions, all of which matter later:

1. **Fasting is occasion-sensitive.** It is right or wrong according to what is true at the time. The same act that is fitting on one day is incongruous on another.
2. **Christian fasting is explicitly predicted.** "Then shall they fast" is not a concession. Jesus states that after he is taken away, his people will fast — the plainest statement in the New Testament that fasting continues into the church age.
3. **It is bracketed by joy on both sides.** The bridegroom has been taken away; he is also coming back. Christian fasting occupies the interval, and that interval has a known end.

Jesus then adds two images that most readers pass over, though they were spoken in this exact context:

> **Matthew 9:16** — No man putteth a piece of new cloth unto an old garment, for that which is put in to fill it up taketh from the garment, and the rent is made worse.

> **Matthew 9:17** — Neither do men put new wine into old bottles: else the bottles break, and the wine runneth out, and the bottles perish: but they put new wine into new bottles, and both are preserved.

The point is that what he brings will not be stitched onto the old religious framework as one more observance. Christian fasting is not Pharisaic fasting with the name of Jesus attached. It needs a new container — a new reason, a new spirit, a new relation to God — and Part III is largely about what that container is.

### He attacked fasting used as a scoreboard

> **Luke 18:11** — The Pharisee stood and prayed thus with himself, God, I thank thee, that I am not as other men are, extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican.

> **Luke 18:12** — I fast twice in the week, I give tithes of all that I possess.

> **Luke 18:14** — I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other: for every one that exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted.

Twice a week — Mondays and Thursdays, by the custom of the day — was serious, sustained, genuinely costly devotion. It was also, by his own account, the reason he believed he compared well. The man who went home justified had fasted nothing and could only say "God be merciful to me a sinner."

Fasting is the discipline most easily turned into a ledger entry, because it is measurable. You can count the meals. The moment the count becomes evidence in your own favour, the discipline has curdled.

### He was not an ascetic

> **Matthew 11:18** — For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil.

> **Matthew 11:19** — The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners. But wisdom is justified of her children.

John fasted severely and was called demonic. Jesus ate and drank and was called a glutton. The passage is primarily about the impossibility of satisfying a determined critic — but it also establishes that Jesus' own habitual practice was not ascetic. He turned water into wine at a wedding; his reputation was built at dinner tables. The forty days in the wilderness were exceptional, and the Gospels record no other fast of his.

Any account of fasting that cannot accommodate a Saviour known for going to parties has drifted from the New Testament.

---

## The early church

The evidence from Acts is real but sparse. Fasting appears at moments of commissioning (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23) and in Cornelius' account of the day he was told to send for Peter (Acts 10:30). Anna the prophetess, at the very start of Luke, "served God with fastings and prayers night and day" (Luke 2:37). Paul lists "fastings" twice among his sufferings:

> **2 Corinthians 6:5** — In stripes, in imprisonments, in tumults, in labours, in watchings, in fastings;

> **2 Corinthians 11:27** — In weariness and painfulness, in watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fastings often, in cold and nakedness.

These two require care. Both sit in lists of hardships, and 11:27 distinguishes "fastings" from "hunger and thirst" in the same breath — which suggests the *fastings* were chosen and the *hunger* was not. Paul, on this reading, fasted often. But the passages are autobiography, not instruction: he is defending his apostleship, not teaching a discipline.

And that is essentially the whole New Testament record outside the Gospels. There is no epistle on fasting, no apostolic command to fast, no instruction on frequency, duration, or method for the churches.

What the earliest post-biblical sources show is that the practice continued and quickly acquired a schedule. The *Didache*, a church manual usually dated to the late first or early second century, tells Christians not to fast on Mondays and Thursdays "with the hypocrites" — the Pharisees' days — but on Wednesdays and Fridays instead. Within a generation of the apostles, the church was fasting twice a week and taking care to do it on different days from the synagogue.

---

## The silence of the epistles — and the warnings

The letters that shape Christian practice on almost everything else say almost nothing about fasting. One verse mentions it in passing:

> **1 Corinthians 7:5** — Defraud ye not one the other, except it be with consent for a time, that ye may give yourselves to fasting and prayer; and come together again, that Satan tempt you not for your incontinency.

— and even here the reference is textually uncertain (see [Appendix C](#c-textual-notes)), and fasting is not the subject of the paragraph.

This silence is not neutral. Set it against what the epistles *do* say when the subject of food and religious observance comes up, and a consistent posture emerges.

**Against food rules as spirituality:**

> **Colossians 2:16** — Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days:

> **Colossians 2:20** — Wherefore if ye be dead with Christ from the rudiments of the world, why, as though living in the world, are ye subject to ordinances,

> **Colossians 2:21** — (Touch not; taste not; handle not;

> **Colossians 2:23** — Which things have indeed a shew of wisdom in will worship, and humility, and neglecting of the body; not in any honour to the satisfying of the flesh.

Colossians 2:23 is the sharpest warning in Scripture about ascetic practice, and its judgement is devastating: such things have a *shew* of wisdom — the appearance, the reputation — in self-imposed worship, humility, and severity to the body, **and are of no value** against the indulgence of the flesh. Not merely insufficient; ineffective at the very thing they advertise. A man can starve his body and feed his pride, and Paul says this is the normal result of religion built on "touch not, taste not."

**Against compulsory abstinence as doctrine:**

> **1 Timothy 4:1** — Now the Spirit speaketh expressly, that in the latter times some shall depart from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits, and doctrines of devils;

> **1 Timothy 4:3** — Forbidding to marry, and commanding to abstain from meats, which God hath created to be received with thanksgiving of them which believe and know the truth.

> **1 Timothy 4:4** — For every creature of God is good, and nothing to be refused, if it be received with thanksgiving:

The phrase is exact and the distinction it draws is the one that matters: **commanding to abstain**. Voluntary abstinence is nowhere condemned in the New Testament. Abstinence *imposed* as a requirement of holiness is classed with doctrines of devils. The line between a discipline and a heresy, in Paul's accounting, runs through whether it is chosen or compelled.

**For liberty in both directions:**

> **Romans 14:3** — Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth: for God hath received him.

> **Romans 14:5** — One man esteemeth one day above another: another esteemeth every day alike. Let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.

> **Romans 14:6** — He that regardeth the day, regardeth it unto the Lord; and he that regardeth not the day, to the Lord he doth not regard it. He that eateth, eateth to the Lord, for he giveth God thanks; and he that eateth not, to the Lord he eateth not, and giveth God thanks.

> **Romans 14:17** — For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost.

Romans 14 refuses to adjudicate. The one who abstains must not judge; the one who eats must not despise. Both are told to act "unto the Lord," and the kingdom is declared to be about something else entirely.

**And the flat valuation:**

> **1 Corinthians 8:8** — But meat commendeth us not to God: for neither, if we eat, are we the better; neither, if we eat not, are we the worse.

> **Hebrews 13:9** — Be not carried about with divers and strange doctrines. For it is a good thing that the heart be established with grace; not with meats, which have not profited them that have been occupied therein.

Taken together these establish the boundary within which everything in Parts II and III must fit:

| The New Testament does | The New Testament does not |
| --- | --- |
| Assume Christians will fast (Matthew 6:16) | Command Christians to fast |
| Predict they will fast after the ascension (Mark 2:20) | Set any frequency, day, or duration |
| Show the church fasting at real decisions (Acts 13:2) | Make fasting a condition of anything |
| Leave it to individual conviction (Romans 14:5) | Permit judging either way about it |
| Warn that abstinence can inflate pride (Colossians 2:23) | Treat abstinence as automatically humble |

Fasting in the New Testament is **expected but not required, valuable but not meritorious, personal but not private-in-the-sense-of-hidden-from-God.** Every practical question in Part II has to be answered inside those brackets.

# Part II — How to Do It

## What Scripture prescribes and what it leaves open

The honest starting point is that the Bible gives far less procedure than most people expect. Laid out plainly:

**Prescribed:**

- Fast in secret, looking normal — *anoint thine head, and wash thy face* (Matthew 6:17)
- Fast toward God, not toward the occasion — *did ye at all fast unto me?* (Zechariah 7:5)
- Let it touch how you treat people, especially the poor (Isaiah 58:6–7)
- Let the heart be rent, not just the garment (Joel 2:13)
- Do not use it to accumulate standing before God or advantage over others (Luke 18:11–14)

**Left open:**

- How often
- How long
- Which day
- Whether food only, or food and water
- Whether alone or with others
- What to abstain from beyond food

The open questions are open on purpose. A required schedule is exactly what 1 Timothy 4:3 warns against and what Colossians 2:16 forbids anyone to impose. So what follows is not a rule but the accumulated shape of how the practice is actually done — Scripture's patterns where it has them, ordinary prudence where it doesn't. Take it as advice, not law, and adapt it.

---

## The kinds of fast found in Scripture

### The normal fast — no food, water continued

The default. Jesus in the wilderness "did eat nothing" (Luke 4:2), and Matthew notes he was hungry afterward — hunger, not thirst, being the deprivation in view. This is what most biblical fasts mean, and it is the right form for nearly everyone.

### The absolute fast — no food or water

Rare, short, and always tied to an emergency. Esther's three days "night or day" (Esther 4:16), Paul's three days of blindness in Damascus (Acts 9:9), Ezra's night of mourning (Ezra 10:6).

**Three days is the biblical outer limit for this, and there is a physiological reason: going without water for longer is dangerous, and past roughly three days it can kill.** Moses' forty days without food *or water* (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 9:9, 9:18) is outside nature and the text presents it that way — he was "there with the LORD" on the mountain. It is not a pattern to copy. No one should attempt an absolute fast beyond a short period, and most people should not attempt one at all.

### The partial fast — restricted diet

Daniel and his companions asking for vegetables and water instead of the king's provision:

> **Daniel 1:12** — Prove thy servants, I beseech thee, ten days; and let them give us pulse to eat, and water to drink.

And Daniel's three weeks of plain food:

> **Daniel 10:3** — I ate no pleasant bread, neither came flesh nor wine in my mouth, neither did I anoint myself at all, till three whole weeks were fulfilled.

Two honest notes. Daniel 1 is not really a fast — it is a refusal to be defiled by the king's table, a loyalty test rather than a discipline, and it ends with the young men looking *better* fed than the rest. Daniel 10 is a genuine partial fast: ordinary food continued, delicacies and wine set aside, for a sustained period.

The partial fast is the right form for a great many people — anyone for whom total abstinence is medically unwise, anyone fasting over weeks rather than hours, anyone whose work is physically demanding. It is fully biblical and nothing about it is second-class.

### The corporate fast

Called by a leader for a community: Jehoshaphat over Judah (2 Chronicles 20:3), Ezra at the river Ahava (Ezra 8:21), the king of Nineveh over his whole city (Jonah 3:7), Joel's summons —

> **Joel 2:15** — Blow the trumpet in Zion, sanctify a fast, call a solemn assembly:

> **Joel 2:16** — Gather the people, sanctify the congregation, assemble the elders, gather the children, and those that suck the breasts: let the bridegroom go forth of his chamber, and the bride out of her closet.

Joel 2:16 is striking for who is included: elders, children, nursing infants, and a bridegroom pulled out of his own wedding. A corporate fast in Scripture suspends normal life across the whole community — which is precisely why it is reserved for genuine crisis and not called routinely.

### The regular fast

Fasting on a fixed schedule. Israel's four exilic fasts (Zechariah 8:19), the Pharisee's twice weekly (Luke 18:12), the *Didache*'s Wednesdays and Fridays. Scripture neither commands nor forbids this. It is worth noticing that both biblical examples of scheduled fasting draw criticism — Zechariah 7:5 and Luke 18:12 — not because a schedule is wrong, but because a schedule is the easiest thing to keep after the meaning has left it.

If you fast on a schedule, the standing question from Zechariah 7:5 is the one to keep asking: *is this unto me?*

---

## Jesus' four rules

Everything Jesus says about the mechanics of fasting is in three verses (Matthew 6:16–18). Four rules come out of them.

**1. Do not look like you are fasting.** *Anoint thine head, and wash thy face.* Groom as usual, behave as usual, do not manufacture a solemn manner. If your fasting is visible, you have swapped the audience.

**2. Do not tell people.** The hypocrites "disfigure their faces, that they may appear unto men to fast." The modern equivalent of disfiguring your face is mentioning it. There are legitimate exceptions — a spouse who shares meals with you, a host expecting you for dinner, a congregation fasting together — and in those cases say the minimum required and move on.

**3. Fast to the Father, who is in secret.** The positive command underneath the two prohibitions. Fasting is not primarily abstaining *from* something; it is attending *to* someone. A fast with nothing at its centre is just being hungry.

**4. Expect the Father to respond.** *Thy Father, which seeth in secret, shall reward thee openly.* Jesus does not say what the reward is or when it comes — and the whole thrust of Matthew 6 is that trying to specify it puts you back among the hypocrites, who knew exactly what they were getting. But he is emphatic that fasting is not shouted into a void.

---

## A practical pattern

### Before

**Settle what it is for.** Every fast in Scripture has an occasion — a grief, a sin, a danger, a decision, a person. Fasting with no object is the one form the Bible never shows. Write the object down in a sentence if that helps: *I am fasting about my father's illness.* *I am fasting over this decision.* *I am fasting because I cannot seem to repent of this and I want to mean it.*

**Set the boundaries in advance.** What you will abstain from, when it starts, when it ends. Deciding mid-fast, while hungry, produces either collapse or escalation, and both are avoidable.

**Start small.** One meal. Then a day. The forty-day fasts in Scripture belong to Moses and Jesus (and to Elijah, by inference — see [Appendix B](#b-the-forty-day-fasts)), and they are not a template. There is no virtue in length as such, and a completed one-day fast is worth more than an abandoned week.

**Plan the time you are freeing up.** This is the step most often skipped and it is the one that decides whether the fast means anything. If you skip lunch and spend the hour on your phone, you have dieted. Decide beforehand what fills the space: prayer for the thing you are fasting about, a psalm, silence, a walk.

**Check whether you should.** See the caution below. This is not a formality.

### During

**Expect it to be unpleasant, and expect the unpleasantness to be informative.** Hunger surfaces things — irritability, anxiety, a startling attachment to comfort. That exposure is part of the point. Isaiah 58:4 warns against letting it spill onto other people; it does not warn against feeling it.

**Drink water.** Unless you are in a short absolute fast for grave reason, keep drinking. Most of what people call "fasting headaches" are dehydration or caffeine withdrawal.

**Pray at the times you would have eaten.** The simplest structure there is, and the most durable. The empty slot in the day becomes the appointment.

**Let hunger be a prompt, not an enemy.** Each pang is a reminder of why you started. This is the mechanism by which a fast becomes prayer rather than endurance: the body keeps raising the subject.

**Do not let it make you unbearable.** *Ye fast for strife and debate* (Isaiah 58:4). If you are snapping at people, the fast is failing at its own purpose. Apologise, and consider whether the length was realistic.

**Give away what you did not spend.** Isaiah 58:7 makes the bread go somewhere. Put the cost of the meals toward someone who needs them. This single practice does more than anything else to keep a fast from turning inward.

### Afterward

**Do not audit it.** Resist grading how it went, and especially resist concluding that God owes an answer. David's *who can tell* (2 Samuel 12:22) is the model. You asked; the answer belongs to God.

**Do not tell people.** The rule does not expire when the fast does. A fast disclosed afterward for effect was, in retrospect, performed.

**Notice what surfaced.** Most of what a fast teaches arrives in the form of things you discovered you could not do without.

---

## Breaking the fast

Break a fast gently. After a day, this scarcely matters; after several days it matters a great deal, because the digestive system slows down and an ordinary meal on an empty stomach can make you genuinely ill. Start with something light — broth, fruit, something small — and return to normal eating over a period roughly proportional to the fast's length. For long fasts, medical guidance on refeeding is not optional.

Scripture has an unexpectedly humane example. Paul, after fourteen days in which no one on the ship had eaten properly, does not call for prayer first:

> **Acts 27:33** — And while the day was coming on, Paul besought them all to take meat, saying, This day is the fourteenth day that ye have tarried and continued fasting, having taken nothing.

He tells them to eat, gives thanks in front of all of them, and eats first. Sometimes the spiritual thing to do is have breakfast.

And David, when the fast was over and the news was bad:

> **2 Samuel 12:20** — Then David arose from the earth, and washed, and anointed himself, and changed his apparel, and came into the house of the LORD, and worshipped: then he came to his own house; and when he required, they set bread before him, and he did eat.

Washed, changed, worshipped, ate. No collapse, no recrimination. That is how a fast ends.

---

## A necessary caution

Fasting from food is a physical act, and for some people it is genuinely unsafe. Scripture nowhere asks anyone to damage their body, and God is not honoured by an injury.

**Do not fast from food without medical advice if any of the following apply.** This list is not exhaustive:

- Diabetes or any disorder of blood-sugar regulation
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding
- A history of, or current struggle with, an eating disorder — fasting can be a direct trigger, and a religious frame can disguise a relapse as devotion
- Being underweight, or a child or adolescent still growing
- Medication that must be taken with food, or whose dosing depends on regular eating
- Kidney, liver, heart, or adrenal conditions
- Recovery from surgery, illness, or injury
- Older age with frailty or low reserves

**Anyone in these situations can still fast.** The partial fast of Daniel 10 is fully biblical; so is abstaining from something other than food. Someone who cannot safely skip meals but gives up an evening's entertainment for the same hours of prayer has done a real thing and is not fasting at a discount.

Two further warnings.

**Fasting is a poor vehicle for a body you already dislike.** If the appeal of a fast is partly that you would like to lose weight, or partly that hunger feels like control, the discipline is being loaded with something it cannot carry, and it will do harm. This is not a hypothetical overlap; it is a well-documented one. Colossians 2:23 is blunt that "neglecting of the body" can look like wisdom and be worth nothing.

**Stop if something is wrong.** Faintness, confusion, palpitations, or sustained vomiting mean the fast ends now. Ending a fast early is not a spiritual failure. There is no biblical text that makes completion the point.

---

## Eight common mistakes

**1. Fasting to obtain instead of fasting to attend.** The Isaiah 58:3 complaint — *wherefore have we fasted, and thou seest not?* Fasting is not a payment. When it becomes one, it curdles into resentment the moment the answer is no.

**2. Fasting from food while leaving everything else untouched.** The people in Isaiah 58 skipped meals and went on driving their workers. A fast that changes nothing about how you treat anyone is the exact fast God rejected.

**3. Making it visible.** Directly against Matthew 6:16–18, and the most common mistake by a wide margin, because the temptation is so mild — just a mention, just an explanation.

**4. Treating length as merit.** Scripture never praises a fast for its duration. The forty-day fasts are descriptions of extraordinary events, not a target.

**5. Filling the freed time with nothing.** A fast with no prayer attached is a diet with a religious label.

**6. Requiring it of others.** "Commanding to abstain from meats" (1 Timothy 4:3). Invite, share, call a corporate fast if you have the standing to — but the moment fasting becomes a test of whether someone is serious about God, it has crossed a line the New Testament draws firmly.

**7. Judging in either direction.** Romans 14:3 forbids both moves: the faster despising the eater and the eater dismissing the faster. Both are common; both are named.

**8. Fasting instead of obeying.** The oldest substitution in the book. Samuel's "to obey is better than sacrifice" applies exactly. If there is a thing you know to do, do it — a fast is not an acceptable alternative to it, and Isaiah 58 exists to say so.

# Part III — Where It Fits in a Christian Life

## Fasting earns nothing

This has to be said first, because every other question depends on it.

Fasting does not make God more willing to hear you. It does not purchase answers, accumulate credit, or improve your standing. The New Testament's position on how anyone stands before God is settled and uniform: by grace, through faith, on account of Christ, not of works. Fasting is a work. If it contributed anything to acceptance, the whole argument of Galatians would collapse.

Scripture makes this hard to miss even in the fasting passages themselves:

- The man who fasted twice a week went home **unjustified**; the man who fasted nothing went home justified (Luke 18:14).
- The people of Isaiah 58 fasted diligently and God refused it.
- Jeremiah 14:12 has God announce in advance that he will not hear their fasting.
- *"Meat commendeth us not to God: for neither, if we eat, are we the better; neither, if we eat not, are we the worse"* (1 Corinthians 8:8).

Then why do it?

Because fasting is not a payment, it is a **posture**. Consider what the act actually is. You are hungry, and you have deliberately declined to fix it. Several hours in, your body is making a case with real force: *this is unnecessary, you could end this, there is food.* And you do not. Not because hunger is good, but because for these hours something else has been given priority.

That is not a transaction with God. It is a rehearsal of a truth you already believe and do not feel — that you do not live by bread alone. The reason Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 8:3 in the wilderness is that Deuteronomy 8:3 is about exactly this:

> **Deuteronomy 8:3** — And he humbled thee, and suffered thee to hunger, and fed thee with manna, which thou knewest not, neither did thy fathers know; that he might make thee know that man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the LORD doth man live.

*That he might make thee know.* Israel was made hungry in order to learn something that could not be learned while full. Fasting is the voluntary version of that wilderness: a self-imposed and temporary condition in which a conviction gets tested against the body's objection.

Which also explains why fasting cannot be assessed by how it feels. Hebrews 12:11 says of all discipline that it does not seem joyous at the time — grievous, rather — and yields its fruit *afterward*. Nobody has ever enjoyed being hungry. That is not evidence the fast is failing.

---

## The bridegroom logic: why Christians fast at all

Jesus' bridegroom saying (Matthew 9:15; Mark 2:19–20) is the most important thing in the New Testament for locating fasting in a Christian life, because it gives fasting a *reason* rather than a rule.

The disciples did not fast, because the bridegroom was there. Fasting, then, belongs to absence. It is what love does when the one loved is not present.

Christians live in a strange position with respect to that. The bridegroom has been taken away — he ascended — and he is also present by his Spirit, and he is also coming back. All three are true at once, and Christian existence is stretched across them. Paul describes the same condition without mentioning fasting at all:

> **Romans 8:23** — And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.

*Firstfruits* and *groaning* in one sentence. Something real has already been given, and it is not yet the whole thing, and the gap between them aches.

Fasting is that ache made deliberate. It is a way of saying with your body what is true and easily forgotten: *this is not yet the wedding feast.* There is a hunger that food does not answer, and fasting is the practice of declining, briefly, to paper over it.

This is why Christian fasting is not sad in the way the Pharisees' fasting was sad. It has a known end. The bridegroom was taken away, and he is coming back, and the fast ends at the table (Revelation 19:9). A Christian fasts the way you wait for someone whose train is due — not the way you mourn someone who is gone for good.

And it is why fasting cannot be the dominant note. The New Testament church is marked by joy, by meals, by a sacrament that is a *supper*. Christians who fast are fasting *between* feasts, and if the fasting swallows the feasting, the shape has gone wrong.

---

## Fasting and prayer

Fasting and prayer are joined so consistently in Scripture that the pair is nearly a single idea: Anna (Luke 2:37), Nehemiah (1:4), Daniel (9:3), Antioch (Acts 13:3), the elders (Acts 14:23), Cornelius (Acts 10:30).

The relationship is best understood as **intensification, not addition**. Fasting does not add a second item to prayer. It concentrates prayer — clears the time, quiets the distractions, and hands you a recurring physical reminder of what you came to ask. The fast does not speak. It makes room for you to.

This bears on a question people ask directly: *does fasting make prayer more effective?*

The honest answer has two halves, and both are in the text.

**It is not a lever.** Isaiah's people fasted and were refused. Jeremiah's were refused in advance. David fasted for a week and the child died. Whatever fasting does, it does not oblige God, and any teaching that presents it as a method for securing outcomes is selling something Scripture does not stock.

**And yet Scripture keeps showing it attached to God's response.** Nineveh fasted and was spared. Ezra fasted and "he was intreated of us." Jehoshaphat fasted and was delivered. Antioch fasted and the Spirit spoke. The pattern is too frequent to wave off, and the plain reading of these texts is that God took notice.

The reconciliation is not difficult once fasting stops being framed as currency. A father is not bribed by his child's seriousness, and is not indifferent to it either. Fasting does not change God's disposition; it does change the person praying — from casually interested to wholly engaged — and Scripture treats that change as something God honours. The promise in Matthew 6:18 is real: *thy Father, which seeth in secret, shall reward thee openly.* It is simply not a promise you get to itemise in advance.

---

## Fasting and the poor

If Isaiah 58 is taken seriously, this is not an optional application but the chapter's main point, and it is the single most neglected element of modern fasting.

> **Isaiah 58:7** — Is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house? when thou seest the naked, that thou cover him; and that thou hide not thyself from thine own flesh?

Fasting has an obvious and under-used property: it produces a surplus. Meals not eaten are money not spent. In the fast God rejects, that surplus simply stays put. In the fast he chooses, it moves.

The practical form is simple enough to state in a sentence: **calculate what the fast saved and give it away.** Historically this was taken for granted — the money saved by Lenten abstinence was alms, not savings — and its disappearance from ordinary practice is a real loss.

There is a second effect, harder to measure and possibly more important. Going hungry on purpose puts you, briefly and at a safe distance, in the condition that a great many people occupy without choosing it. That is not the same as poverty and should never be confused with it. But it is not nothing. *Hide not thyself from thine own flesh* — Isaiah's phrase for refusing to look away from people who are like you — is easier to obey from inside a hunger you have felt.

---

## Fasting and the body

Christianity is not an ascetic religion, and this has to be stated clearly because fasting sits so close to the border.

The body is made by God and called good. The Son took one. The resurrection is bodily. The hope is not escape from the body but "the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23). Every creature of God is good, and nothing to be refused (1 Timothy 4:4). Paul's charge against the false teachers is precisely that they treated matter as the problem.

So fasting is **not** the spirit punishing the flesh, and Christian fasting has nothing in common with the idea that the body is a prison or an enemy. Colossians 2:23 aims squarely at the version that thinks it is: severity to the body has a reputation for wisdom and accomplishes nothing.

What fasting does is different and more modest. It is the body being *taught* rather than punished — brought into the conversation, made to participate in a conviction rather than merely be carried along by one. Paul's own image is athletic, not penal:

> **1 Corinthians 9:27** — But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway.

A runner in training is not at war with their legs.

The test for whether a fast has gone wrong here is straightforward: it is going wrong if it is generating contempt — for food, for your body, or for people who are not fasting. Gratitude is the New Testament's fixed mark of a right relation to food (1 Timothy 4:4; Romans 14:6), and a fast that leaves you less grateful at the next meal has not worked.

The right instinct is Paul's:

> **Philippians 4:12** — I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need.

Full and hungry, both learned, neither feared.

---

## Freedom, not law

Because fasting is not commanded, several things follow, and they go in both directions.

**No one may require it of you.** Not a church, not a leader, not a movement. "Commanding to abstain from meats" is what 1 Timothy 4:3 names as a mark of departure from the faith, and Colossians 2:16 forbids anyone to judge you in meat or drink.

**You may not require it of anyone else.** Including, with some effort, of yourself in the sense of turning a chosen practice into a private law whose breach is guilt. If you fast weekly and one week you don't, nothing has happened. There is no debt.

**You may not despise those who don't.** Romans 14:3, the first half.

**And they may not despise you.** Romans 14:3, the second half — the half that gets less airtime. A Christian who fasts is not being excessive, medieval, or performative by default. Jesus assumed his disciples would do this.

**A church may commend it without commanding it.** The distinction is exactly the one the New Testament draws. Calling a congregation to fast over a real crisis is biblical (Joel 2:15; Acts 13:2–3). Making fasting a measure of who is serious is not.

Romans 14:5 gives the governing principle, and it is unusually permissive: *let every man be fully persuaded in his own mind.* Not "let every man do as he pleases" — *persuaded*, which requires actually thinking it through — but the conclusion is genuinely left to the individual before God.

---

## Feast and fast

The Bible's calendar is not a fasting calendar. Leviticus 23 sets out Israel's year: seven appointed times, most of them feasts, several running a full week — and exactly **one** of them a fast. The ratio is not accidental, and it is the opposite of the impression most people carry about Old Testament religion.

The New Testament keeps the proportion. Jesus' first sign is at a wedding, his critics call him a glutton and a drinker, the kingdom is a banquet in his parables, the sacrament he left is a meal, and the last image in Scripture is a wedding supper. Fasting is real, and it is the minor key.

So fasting belongs in a Christian life the way a minor key belongs in music: it is not the whole piece, it is not an interruption of the piece, and a piece without it is thinner than it should be. A Christian who never fasts has probably never physically registered the not-yet. A Christian who is always fasting has misread which age they are in.

The rhythm Scripture suggests is roughly this. Feasting is the default, because the bridegroom has come, the tomb is empty, and the Spirit is given. Fasting arrives with occasions — a grief, a decision, a sin that won't shift, a danger, a time of seeking. It is intense, it is bounded, and it ends with washing your face and eating something.

Zechariah 8:19 is where the whole subject is going. The four fasts of the exile were to become "joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts." Not abolished — *converted*. Every fast a Christian keeps is provisional in exactly that way, pointed at a table that is already set.

## How the church has practised it

A brief sketch, offered as context rather than authority. Christians have disagreed about this for most of two thousand years, and knowing the shape of the disagreement helps.

**The early centuries.** Fasting was universal and quickly regularised. The *Didache* has Wednesdays and Fridays. A short fast before Easter grew, by the fourth century, into the forty days of Lent — the number taken from Moses, Elijah, and Jesus. Fasts before baptism and ordination echo Acts 13.

**The medieval West.** Fasting became a detailed legal system: prescribed days, prescribed foods, dispensations, and — the part the Reformers objected to most — a widespread assumption that fasting was meritorious, capable of making satisfaction for sin.

**The Reformation.** The Reformers did not abolish fasting; they attacked its compulsion and its merit. Calvin devotes a careful section of the *Institutes* (IV.xii) to it, defends it as a genuine aid to prayer and repentance, and then names three dangers with some heat: treating it as meritorious, treating the outward act as the thing itself, and requiring it by law. Luther kept fasting as personal discipline and rejected it as obligation. Protestant churches through the seventeenth century regularly called national days of fasting in crisis — the corporate biblical pattern, without the calendar.

**Later Protestantism.** John Wesley kept a weekly fast — Fridays, and in his Oxford years Wednesdays too — expected the same of his preachers, and called fasting a means of grace — while insisting, as the Reformers had, that it merits nothing. In much of modern evangelicalism the practice quietly disappeared, and its recovery in the last several decades is genuine but uneven, and sometimes pitched in the language of technique and breakthrough that Isaiah 58 was written against.

The consistent thread across all of it: the practice survives, and the arguments are always about the same two things — whether it can be commanded, and whether it earns anything. The New Testament answers both, and answers them no.

## B. The forty-day fasts

Three figures in Scripture are associated with forty days without food, and the pattern is worth setting out — including one common misreading.

| Who | Reference | What the text says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Moses** | Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 9:9, 18 | Forty days and nights, no bread **and no water**, twice — at the giving of the covenant and after the golden calf |
| **Elijah** | 1 Kings 19:8 | Travelled forty days and nights "in the strength of that meat" — *the text does not say he fasted* |
| **Jesus** | Matthew 4:2; Luke 4:2 | Forty days, ate nothing, "he afterward hungered" |

**On Elijah.** He is routinely listed as one of the Bible's forty-day fasters, but 1 Kings 19:8 says something slightly different: an angel fed him twice, and on the strength of that food he journeyed forty days to Horeb. The text says nothing about whether he ate along the way. The parallel with Moses and Jesus is real — all three are at Sinai/Horeb or in the wilderness, all three are at a hinge of redemptive history — but the fast is an inference, not a statement.

**On the significance.** Forty days is the number of Israel's forty years in the wilderness, shortened to a lifetime's worth of testing in a single season. Moses fasts as the covenant is given; Jesus fasts as the new covenant's work begins, is tempted where Israel failed, and answers each temptation from Deuteronomy — the book of the wilderness. The forty days are not a fasting record. They are a claim about who Jesus is.

**On imitation.** Two of the three are supernaturally sustained — Moses' forty days without water is not survivable — and all three are unique events at unique junctures. These are not a template. Nobody in Scripture attempts a forty-day fast in imitation of anyone else.

## C. Textual notes

Four of the New Testament's fasting references are textually uncertain: they appear in the KJV and the Greek manuscripts behind it, but are absent from the oldest surviving manuscripts, and modern critical editions omit or bracket them. Most modern translations footnote all four.

| Passage | The issue |
| --- | --- |
| **Matthew 17:21** | The entire verse — "Howbeit this kind goeth not out but by prayer and fasting" — is absent from the earliest manuscripts. It is generally judged a copyist's assimilation to Mark 9:29, and modern translations omit it or relegate it to a footnote. |
| **Mark 9:29** | The words **"and fasting"** are absent from the earliest manuscripts. The critical text reads "This kind can come forth by nothing, but by prayer." |
| **Acts 10:30** | The word **"fasting"** in Cornelius' account is absent from the major early manuscripts, which read simply "Four days ago, until this hour, I was praying." |
| **1 Corinthians 7:5** | The words **"fasting and"** are absent from the earliest manuscripts, which read "that ye may give yourselves to prayer." |

Two observations, which cut in opposite directions and should both be held.

**A real pattern.** All four variants add fasting rather than remove it. The most economical explanation is that later scribes, in a church that fasted regularly, supplied it where prayer was mentioned. This should make anyone cautious about building doctrine on these four verses in particular — and Matthew 17:21 and Mark 9:29 are, between them, the most frequently cited proof-texts for fasting as spiritual power over demonic opposition.

**Nothing structural depends on them.** Remove all four entirely and the biblical case for fasting is essentially unchanged. Matthew 6:16–18 is not disputed. Neither is the bridegroom saying in all three Synoptics, nor Acts 13:2–3, nor Acts 14:23, nor Isaiah 58, nor Joel 2. The four contested verses affect the question of what fasting *achieves*; they do not affect whether Christians fast.

A note on the KJV itself: it rests on the Byzantine/*Textus Receptus* tradition, which includes all four readings. Christians differ, sometimes sharply, over which text-form to prefer. This document uses the KJV for licensing reasons and flags the variants so that readers in either camp can see exactly what is and is not contested.

## D. Honest caveats

**The biblical material is thinner than the literature on it.** Roughly sixty verses, no epistle, no command, no schedule. Books and sermons on fasting routinely present a level of detail the Bible does not supply. Where this document gives practical guidance in Part II, most of it is prudence and accumulated practice, not revelation, and it is labelled that way.

**"When ye fast" is an assumption, not a command.** The argument from Matthew 6:16 is strong — Jesus plainly expects his disciples to fast — but it is an argument from expectation. Christians who conclude that fasting is therefore obligatory are going further than the text, and Romans 14 stands against making it a requirement.

**The categories in Part I overlap.** Grief, repentance, crisis, intercession, guidance, and consecration are analytical conveniences. Nehemiah's fast is all six at once. Any taxonomy of this material is imposed on it.

**Whether fasting "moves" God is genuinely disputed.** Some texts show God responding to fasts (Jonah 3:10; Ezra 8:23; 1 Kings 21:29). Others show him refusing them (Isaiah 58:3; Jeremiah 14:12), and one shows a righteous fast answered with no (2 Samuel 12). Part III offers a reading that holds these together; readers who weigh the passages differently will land elsewhere, and the texts on both sides are quoted here so they can.

**"Fasting" from things other than food is an extension.** The biblical vocabulary means food. Abstaining from media, entertainment, or spending for prayer's sake is a legitimate and often wise application of the same logic — and it is the right option for those who cannot safely fast from food — but it should not be presented as the plain meaning of the biblical term.

**The historical sketch is a sketch.** Two thousand years in eight sentences omits nearly everything: the Desert Fathers, the Eastern churches' fasting seasons, monastic rules, the Puritan fast days, the Catholic reforms of the twentieth century. It is oriented to the two questions the New Testament settles, not offered as history.

**Health guidance here is general.** The caution in Part II is not medical advice and cannot account for any individual's circumstances. Anyone with a medical condition, taking regular medication, pregnant, or with a history of disordered eating should speak to a doctor before fasting from food.
