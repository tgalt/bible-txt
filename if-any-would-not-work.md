# "If Any Would Not Work"
## The One Verse That Says It, What It Actually Commands, and the Whole of Scripture Around It

---

## The short answer

**Yes. There is exactly one verse, and it is 2 Thessalonians 3:10.**

> 2 Thessalonians 3:10 — For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat.

It means what it appears to mean, and four things about it are usually missed:

1. **"Would not" means "is not willing to."** The Greek is *ou thelei ergazesthai* — "does not will to work," "refuses to work." The verse is about a settled refusal, not about unemployment, disability, illness, age, a bad harvest, or a shut factory. Paul's rule has a built-in exemption for everyone who wants work and cannot get it, and he never states the exemption because the wording already carries it.

2. **It is a rule for a church about its own members, not a policy about the poor.** The paragraph begins "withdraw yourselves from every **brother** that walketh disorderly" (v. 6). Paul is telling a congregation how to handle men inside it who were living off the common table while refusing to work. He is not addressing what a society owes its poor, and he is not addressing strangers at all.

3. **It is corrective discipline aimed at restoration, and Paul says so.** Five verses later: "count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" (v. 15). The point of withholding is to shame a man back to work, not to be rid of him.

4. **Paul immediately forbids the obvious misuse.** Three verses after the rule comes the guard on it: "But ye, brethren, be not weary in well doing" (v. 13). Having just authorised one refusal, he tells them not to let it cool their generosity generally. Chrysostom noticed the sequence in the fourth century and said Paul added it "fearing lest they should perish by hunger."

So the verse is real, it is binding, and it is narrow. It licenses a church to stop subsidising a member's chosen idleness. It licenses nothing else, and the rest of Scripture — which commands open-handed giving to the poor without a worthiness test, more often and more emphatically than it commands anything about idleness — stands untouched by it.

The rest of this document shows the work.

### What this document is and is not

This is a study of what the text says and how it has been used. On the central exegetical question — does 2 Thessalonians 3:10 restrict charity generally, or only a church's support of its own idle members — the commentators are close to unanimous for the second reading, and this document says so and shows why. On the political question the verse is usually dragged into, this document takes no side, because the verse does not take one. What it does is mark the line between what the verse settles and what it leaves open, so that neither side can borrow authority it does not have.

# Part I — The Verse

## 1. The verse

> 2 Thessalonians 3:10 — For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat.

Second Thessalonians is a short letter, probably written from Corinth around AD 51–52, within months of First Thessalonians, to a young congregation in the Macedonian capital that Paul had founded on his second journey and had been driven out of in a hurry (Acts 17:1–10). The letter has three chapters. The first comforts them under persecution, the second corrects a panic about the Day of the Lord, and the third — this one — deals with a discipline problem.

The sentence is the only place in the Bible where anything of this kind is said. There is no Old Testament parallel, no saying of Jesus to this effect, and no second apostolic statement of it. Whatever weight the principle carries, it carries on this one verse and its paragraph.

Notice also how Paul introduces it: *"even when we were with you, this we commanded you."* He is not announcing a new rule. He is reminding them of a standing one, given orally during the founding visit, evidently more than once — the Greek verb is in the imperfect, the tense of repeated past action: *we used to charge you this*. It was part of the basic instruction of the church from the beginning.

---

## 2. The whole paragraph

The verse cannot be read alone. Here is the unit it belongs to, entire:

> 2 Thessalonians 3:6 — Now we command you, brethren, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that ye withdraw yourselves from every brother that walketh disorderly, and not after the tradition which he received of us.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:7 — For yourselves know how ye ought to follow us: for we behaved not ourselves disorderly among you;

> 2 Thessalonians 3:8 — Neither did we eat any man's bread for nought; but wrought with labour and travail night and day, that we might not be chargeable to any of you:

> 2 Thessalonians 3:9 — Not because we have not power, but to make ourselves an ensample unto you to follow us.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:10 — For even when we were with you, this we commanded you, that if any would not work, neither should he eat.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:11 — For we hear that there are some which walk among you disorderly, working not at all, but are busybodies.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:12 — Now them that are such we command and exhort by our Lord Jesus Christ, that with quietness they work, and eat their own bread.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:13 — But ye, brethren, be not weary in well doing.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:14 — And if any man obey not our word by this epistle, note that man, and have no company with him, that he may be ashamed.

> 2 Thessalonians 3:15 — Yet count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.

Ten verses, and the shape of the argument is clear:

| Verse | Move |
| --- | --- |
| 6 | The command: withdraw from a disorderly **brother** |
| 7–9 | The precedent: Paul worked for his own bread, though he had the right not to |
| 10 | The rule: the unwilling do not eat |
| 11 | The diagnosis: some among you are doing no work and minding everyone's business |
| 12 | The remedy, addressed to them: work quietly and eat your own bread |
| 13 | The guard, addressed to everyone else: do not get tired of doing good |
| 14–15 | The procedure: mark him, withdraw company, aim at his shame — and his restoration |

Verse 10 is a premise inside an argument about church discipline. It is not a free-standing maxim about poverty, and every one of the nine verses around it is about a member of the congregation.

---

## 3. The Greek

> 2 Thessalonians 3:10 (Scrivener's Textus Receptus) — και γαρ οτε ημεν προς υμας τουτο παρηγγελλομεν υμιν οτι ει τις ου θελει εργαζεσθαι μηδε εσθιετω

> 2 Thessalonians 3:10 (SBLGNT, accented) — καὶ γὰρ ὅτε ἦμεν πρὸς ὑμᾶς, τοῦτο παρηγγέλλομεν ὑμῖν, ὅτι εἴ τις οὐ θέλει ἐργάζεσθαι μηδὲ ἐσθιέτω.

Four words carry the weight.

### θέλει (*thelei*) — "would"

The pivot of the whole verse. Strong's G2309, θέλω:

> **G2309 θέλω** *thélō* — to determine (as an active option from subjective impulse; whereas G1014 βούλομαι properly denotes rather a passive acquiescence in objective considerations), i.e. choose or prefer … by implication, to wish, i.e. be inclined to
> *KJV renderings:* desire, be disposed (forward), intend, list, love, mean, please, have rather, (be) will (have, -ling, -ling(-ly))

The clause is *ei tis ou thelei ergazesthai* — literally "if anyone is-not-willing to-be-working." The negative attaches to the willing, not to the working. The condition is not *if a man is not working* but *if a man will not work.* A man out of work is not in view. A man who has decided not to work is.

The KJV's "would not" is an exact rendering of this in seventeenth-century English, where *would* is the past of *will* in its old sense of *to wish, to be willing* — the same sense preserved in "he would not hear of it." Modern versions make it explicit rather than more accurate: NASB and NIV have "is not willing to work" / "unwilling to work," ESV "is not willing to work." Every major translation agrees, because the Greek leaves no room to disagree.

This one word does all the qualifying work in the verse. It is why nobody has to append "unless he is sick" — the sick man does not fall under a clause about the unwilling in the first place.

### ἐσθιέτω (*esthietō*) — "should he eat"

A third-person present imperative: *let him not be eating.* English has no clean third-person imperative, which is why the KJV reaches for "neither should he eat" and modern versions for "let him not eat."

Two things follow from the form. First, it is a *standing* imperative — present tense, describing an ongoing state of affairs, not a one-time act. Second, although it is grammatically addressed to the man, it functions as an instruction to the community, because only the community can carry it out. Calvin put the point exactly: Paul "does not mean that he gave commandment to those persons, but forbade that the Thessalonians should encourage their indolence by supplying them with food."

What is being withheld is the church's bread — the common meal, the congregational dole, the hospitality of the brothers. Verse 12 makes the contrast: they are to "eat their own bread." The sanction is the removal of *support*, not the imposition of starvation.

### ἀτάκτως (*ataktōs*) — "disorderly" (vv. 6, 7, 11)

> **G0813 ἄτακτος** *átaktos* — from Α (as a negative particle) and a derivative of G5021 τάσσω; unarranged, i.e. (by implication) insubordinate (religiously) — *KJV:* unruly
> **G0814 ἀτάκτως** *atáktōs* — adverb from ἄτακτος, irregularly (morally) — *KJV:* disorderly
> **G0812 ἀτακτέω** *ataktéō* — from ἄτακτος; to be (i.e. act) irregular — *KJV:* behave self disorderly

The word group runs through the paragraph four times, and one of the four is about Paul: "we behaved not ourselves disorderly among you" in v. 7 is *ouk ētaktēsamen*, the verb. He applies the charge to himself first, in the negative, before he applies it to anyone else.

The root is military: τάσσω is to draw up in formation, and ἄτακτος is out of rank, broken formation, the soldier who will not keep step. The papyri push it further in exactly the direction the passage needs. The word group turns up in Greek apprenticeship contracts, where a father guarantees that his son will make good any days on which he is *atakt-* — that is, plays truant from the workshop. George Milligan drew attention to this in his 1908 commentary on the Thessalonian letters, and it has shaped every commentary since: the word in this context is not merely "disorderly" in general but specifically "shirking."

That is why the modern translations render it "idle" (NIV) or "in idleness" (ESV, RSV) rather than the KJV's "disorderly." Both are defensible; "disorderly" is the broader and older sense, "idle" the sense the context requires. The KJV's "unruly" at 1 Thessalonians 5:14 translates the adjective from the same root — and that verse, as section 18 and Appendix A note, is the one place Paul spells out that the unruly and the weak get opposite treatment.

### περιεργαζομένους (*periergazomenous*) — "busybodies" (v. 11)

Paul makes a pun, and the KJV cannot reproduce it:

> μηδὲν ἐργαζομένους ἀλλὰ περιεργαζομένους
> *mēden ergazomenous alla periergazomenous*
> "not working at all, but working all around"

> **G4020 περιεργάζομαι** *periergázomai* — from περί and ἐργάζομαι; to work all around, i.e. bustle about (meddle) — *KJV:* be a busybody

They are not *ergazomenoi* (workers) but *peri-ergazomenoi* (around-workers). Moffatt's rendering is the best known attempt: "busybodies instead of busy." The observation behind the joke is that the man who has nothing to do finds something: other people's affairs. Paul's prescription in v. 12 answers both halves — work (against the idleness) and do it "with quietness," ἡσυχία (against the meddling).

### One word that is absent

There is no word for *poor* anywhere in the passage. Not πτωχός, not πένης, not ἐνδεής. Paul never once frames this as a question about the poor. He frames it as a question about the disorderly.

## 4. Who "any" is

The English "if any" (*ei tis*) sounds universal. It is not, because it is governed by the sentence that opened the paragraph:

> 2 Thessalonians 3:6 — … that ye withdraw yourselves from every brother that walketh disorderly …

and by the one that follows it:

> 2 Thessalonians 3:11 — For we hear that there are some which walk among you disorderly …

and by the one that closes it:

> 2 Thessalonians 3:15 — Yet count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.

Three words fix the scope of the paragraph — *brother*, *among you*, *brother*. The person in view is a baptised member of the Thessalonian congregation, known by name to the people Paul is writing to, drawing on the church's resources, and subject to the church's discipline. "If any" means "if any of you."

This is not a technicality. It determines the whole reach of the verse. The three things that make the rule workable in Thessalonica are all missing outside that setting:

1. **They knew him.** The church could tell the difference between a man who would not work and a man who could not, because they lived in the same small city and could see.
2. **He had accepted the obligations of membership.** He had received "the tradition which he received of us" (v. 6) — the instruction that included this very rule. He was being held to something he had signed up for.
3. **They wanted him back.** The withdrawal has a stated purpose — "that he may be ashamed" (v. 14) — and a stated limit — "count him not as an enemy" (v. 15). It is a measure taken *toward* a person, not against him.

Strip those away and you no longer have Paul's situation. A stranger asking for money is not a brother who received the tradition; a policy applied to thousands is not a congregation that knows a man's face; and a rule enforced by people who would be glad to see the back of him is not discipline aimed at restoration. The verse can still instruct in those settings — by analogy, with care — but it does not straightforwardly *apply*, and anyone who quotes it as though it does is quoting past its own terms.

## 5. What was actually wrong at Thessalonica

Paul tells us what they were doing — not working, meddling, eating other people's bread — but not why. Three explanations have been offered, and it is worth knowing all three, because they lead to the same place.

**The eschatological reading (the traditional one).** They had stopped working because they thought the end had come. The previous chapter of the same letter deals with exactly this confusion:

> 2 Thessalonians 2:2 — That ye be not soon shaken in mind, or be troubled, neither by spirit, nor by word, nor by letter as from us, as that the day of Christ is at hand.

On this reading, the idlers were the practical wing of the panic in chapter 2: if the Lord returns next week, why plant a crop? The objection is that Paul never explicitly joins the two — chapter 3 gives no eschatological reason for the idleness — but the letter's own agenda makes the connection natural.

**The patron–client reading.** Bruce Winter argued in *Seek the Welfare of the City* (1994) that the idlers were behaving like Roman *clientes*: poorer men attached to a wealthy patron, whose day was spent in attendance on him rather than in a trade, and who received a dole in return. A convert of that class joining a church with a common purse would simply have transferred the arrangement — swapping the patron's handout for the congregation's, and the patron's atrium for the brothers' houses, with the same fundamental refusal to earn. On this reading, "wandering about from house to house" (the parallel idea in 1 Timothy 5:13) is not aimless drifting but the client's daily round.

**The social-contempt reading.** In the Greek cities, manual labour carried real stigma among those with any pretension to status. Trades were *banausic* — fit for slaves and the low-born — and a man with a patron or a little money might well regard picking up a tool as a step down. The idlers, on this view, were not fanatics or spongers but snobs: they did not think they *should* have to work.

**Which is right matters less than what they share.** In all three, the people Paul disciplines are able to work and have decided not to. Not one of the three reconstructions involves a person who cannot find work, cannot do work, or has no work to do. The interpretive debate is about the motive for a refusal that everybody agrees was a refusal.

There is one more thing all three share. In every version, the idlers were being fed — by the church, by patrons, by the brothers' hospitality. That is the practice Paul is stopping. A man who was not being fed by anyone would not have been a problem Paul had to write a letter about.

## 6. Six things the verse does not say

Stated plainly, because each of these is regularly read into it.

**1. It does not say the unemployed should not eat.** The condition is unwillingness. A man who wants work and has none does not satisfy it. Paul does not need to make an exception for him because he was never included.

**2. It does not say the sick, disabled, elderly, or dependent should not eat.** Same reason. And when the New Testament does address people who cannot support themselves, it does the opposite — it commands the church to fund them (1 Timothy 5:3–16, section 16 below).

**3. It does not say "do not give to the poor."** The word *poor* never appears. The verse is about a specific man's refusal, not about a condition of life. Reading it as a general rule about poverty requires importing a subject the passage does not have.

**4. It does not say a nation should not have public relief.** Paul is writing to a congregation of perhaps a few dozen people about their own common table, in a city where they had no political power whatever. Any inference from that to the welfare policy of a modern state is an argument someone has to make — it may even be a good argument — but it is not something the verse says. The verse is evidence in such an argument; it is not the conclusion.

**5. It does not authorise anyone to starve anybody.** What the church withholds is *its own support*. Paul does not tell them to prevent him earning, drive him out of the city, stop others helping him, or let him die. He tells them to stop supplying him, so that hunger does its ordinary work of making a man go to work — and he assumes the man has that option. Where the option does not exist, the mechanism Paul is relying on does not exist either.

**6. It does not license contempt.** "Count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" (v. 15) is part of the same command. A refusal given with scorn is not obedience to this passage; it is disobedience to the last verse of it.

---

## 7. What Paul actually commands the church to do

The passage prescribes a procedure. It is worth setting out, because it is a good deal more careful than the way the verse is usually deployed.

**Step one: work yourself, first.** Paul spends verses 7–9 on his own example before he gives any instruction about anyone else. He supported himself by his trade — he was a tentmaker (Acts 18:3) — through the whole Thessalonian mission:

> 1 Thessalonians 2:9 — For ye remember, brethren, our labour and travail: for labouring night and day, because we would not be chargeable unto any of you, we preached unto you the gospel of God.

And he is careful to say that he had every right to be supported and waived it deliberately: "Not because we have not power, but to make ourselves an ensample unto you to follow us" (v. 9). Paul elsewhere insists that ministers *should* be paid (1 Corinthians 9:14; Galatians 6:6; 1 Timothy 5:18), and he took money from other churches while working in Thessalonica (Philippians 4:16; 2 Corinthians 11:8–9). The self-support at Thessalonica was a teaching device aimed at exactly the problem in this chapter. He earned the right to say verse 10 by first doing verses 7–9.

**Step two: address the man directly.** Verse 12 is spoken to the idlers themselves, in the second person, with the full weight of apostolic authority: "we command and exhort by our Lord Jesus Christ, that with quietness they work, and eat their own bread." The first move is to tell him, plainly, to go to work. The sanction comes only after instruction has failed.

**Step three: stop the subsidy.** Verse 10.

**Step four: mark him and withdraw company.** "Note that man, and have no company with him, that he may be ashamed" (v. 14). This is social, not merely financial — the loss of the church's fellowship, which in that setting was the loss of nearly everything.

**Step five: keep the goal in view.** "That he may be ashamed" — the aim is a change of mind. "Count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" — admonition continues; he is not cast off; the relationship is preserved through the discipline. A church that has stopped speaking to the man has left the procedure, not completed it.

Anyone invoking this passage should be asked which step they are on. In practice the verse is usually cited to skip straight to step three without steps one, two, four, or five — which is not what the passage teaches.

---

## 8. The sentence Paul puts immediately after it

> 2 Thessalonians 3:13 — But ye, brethren, be not weary in well doing.

The Greek phrase is close to the one in Galatians:

> Galatians 6:9 — And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not.

> Galatians 6:10 — As we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith.

Paul knew what a rule like verse 10 would do to a congregation if left unguarded: it would give every tight-fisted man in the room a proof text, and it would make the generous ones suspicious. So he put the guard three verses later, in the same breath, addressed pointedly to the rest of the church — *"But ye, brethren."*

John Chrysostom, preaching through this letter in Antioch in the 390s, read the two sentences as a single movement, and his comment is the sharpest thing anyone has said about the pairing:

> "For as he had said, 'if any does not work neither let him eat,' fearing lest they should perish by hunger, he has added, 'But in doing good, be not ye weary.'"
> — John Chrysostom, *Homily V on 2 Thessalonians* (NPNF translation)

That is the internal balance of the passage, stated by a man whose native language was Greek: the rule is real, and the apostle who gave it was immediately anxious about what it might be used to justify.

# Part II — The Rest of Scripture

One verse does not make a doctrine, and this one sits inside a very large body of teaching about work, money, and the poor. Here is what that teaching says — the parts that support a hard reading and the parts that constrain it.

## 9. Work is good, from the first page

Scripture's estimate of work is high, and it starts before the fall, not after it:

> Genesis 2:15 — And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it.

Adam has a job before he has a problem. What the fall adds is not work but toil:

> Genesis 3:17 — … cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life;

> Genesis 3:19 — In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground …

Genesis 3:19 is the nearest thing in the Old Testament to 2 Thessalonians 3:10, and it is not a command — it is a description of the world as it now is. Work is written into the moral architecture:

> Exodus 20:9 — Six days shalt thou labour, and do all thy work:

The fourth commandment, usually quoted for the rest it commands, commands the labour too. And Scripture treats the fruit of one's own work as a blessing:

> Psalms 128:2 — For thou shalt eat the labour of thine hands: happy shalt thou be, and it shall be well with thee.

> Ephesians 4:28 — Let him that stole steal no more: but rather let him labour, working with his hands the thing which is good, that he may have to give to him that needeth.

Ephesians 4:28 is worth pausing on. The thief is told to work — and the stated goal of his working is not self-sufficiency but *capacity to give*. Scripture's reason for wanting everyone employed is not to reduce the number of people receiving help; it is to increase the number of people able to offer it.

> 1 Thessalonians 4:11 — And that ye study to be quiet, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you;

> 1 Thessalonians 4:12 — That ye may walk honestly toward them that are without, and that ye may have lack of nothing.

> Romans 12:11 — Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord;

> Colossians 3:23 — And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men;

So there is no case for reading 2 Thessalonians 3:10 as an outlier or an embarrassment. It is the sharp end of a consistent biblical estimate of work.

---

## 10. The sluggard in Proverbs

Proverbs has a recurring character — the *sluggard*, the slothful man — and treats him with a mixture of exasperation and comedy. He is the Old Testament's portrait of the person 2 Thessalonians 3:10 is about.

> Proverbs 6:6–8 — Go to the ant, thou sluggard; consider her ways, and be wise: Which having no guide, overseer, or ruler, Provideth her meat in the summer, and gathereth her food in the harvest.

> Proverbs 6:9–11 — How long wilt thou sleep, O sluggard? when wilt thou arise out of thy sleep? Yet a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep: So shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth, and thy want as an armed man.

> Proverbs 19:15 — Slothfulness casteth into a deep sleep; and an idle soul shall suffer hunger.

> Proverbs 20:4 — The sluggard will not plow by reason of the cold; therefore shall he beg in harvest, and have nothing.

> Proverbs 21:25 — The desire of the slothful killeth him; for his hands refuse to labour.

> Proverbs 13:4 — The soul of the sluggard desireth, and hath nothing: but the soul of the diligent shall be made fat.

> Proverbs 14:23 — In all labour there is profit: but the talk of the lips tendeth only to penury.

> Proverbs 18:9 — He also that is slothful in his work is brother to him that is a great waster.

He has excuses, and Proverbs finds them funny:

> Proverbs 22:13 — The slothful man saith, There is a lion without, I shall be slain in the streets.

> Proverbs 26:13–16 — The slothful man saith, There is a lion in the way; a lion is in the streets. As the door turneth upon his hinges, so doth the slothful upon his bed. The slothful hideth his hand in his bosom; it grieveth him to bring it again to his mouth. The sluggard is wiser in his own conceit than seven men that can render a reason.

And the classic set-piece:

> Proverbs 24:30–34 — I went by the field of the slothful, and by the vineyard of the man void of understanding; And, lo, it was all grown over with thorns, and nettles had covered the face thereof, and the stone wall thereof was broken down. Then I saw, and considered it well: I looked upon it, and received instruction. Yet a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to sleep: So shall thy poverty come as one that travelleth; and thy want as an armed man.

The wisdom tradition also puts idleness among the sins of Sodom, which is not where most readers expect to find it:

> Ezekiel 16:49 — Behold, this was the iniquity of thy sister Sodom, pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness was in her and in her daughters, neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy.

Read that verse slowly. Sodom's idleness and Sodom's failure toward the poor are named in the same breath, as a single complex of sin. Ezekiel does not permit the two halves to be separated, and any use of "idleness" from this verse that drops "neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy" is using half a sentence.

## 11. The other half of Proverbs

The same book that mocks the sluggard is severe about the man who will not give.

> Proverbs 19:17 — He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD; and that which he hath given will he pay him again.

> Proverbs 21:13 — Whoso stoppeth his ears at the cry of the poor, he also shall cry himself, but shall not be heard.

> Proverbs 14:31 — He that oppresseth the poor reproacheth his Maker: but he that honoureth him hath mercy on the poor.

> Proverbs 28:27 — He that giveth unto the poor shall not lack: but he that hideth his eyes shall have many a curse.

> Proverbs 22:9 — He that hath a bountiful eye shall be blessed; for he giveth of his bread to the poor.

> Proverbs 3:27–28 — Withhold not good from them to whom it is due, when it is in the power of thine hand to do it. Say not unto thy neighbour, Go, and come again, and to morrow I will give; when thou hast it by thee.

> Psalms 41:1 — Blessed is he that considereth the poor: the LORD will deliver him in time of trouble.

Not one of these attaches a condition. None says "the deserving poor," "the industrious poor," "the poor who are poor through no fault of their own." The wisdom literature is entirely capable of drawing that distinction — it draws it constantly when describing *how people become poor* — and it declines to draw it when commanding *how you must treat them.*

That is the pattern to notice, and it holds right across the canon. **Scripture diagnoses poverty with discrimination and relieves it without.**

## 12. Israel's welfare law, and the shape of it

The Law of Moses had a provision for the poor, and its design is instructive, because it neither hands out nor holds back.

> Leviticus 19:9–10 — And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corners of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleanings of thy harvest. And thou shalt not glean thy vineyard, neither shalt thou gather every grape of thy vineyard; thou shalt leave them for the poor and stranger: I am the LORD your God.

> Deuteronomy 24:19–21 — When thou cuttest down thine harvest in thy field, and hast forgot a sheaf in the field, thou shalt not go again to fetch it: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow: that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thine hands. When thou beatest thine olive tree, thou shalt not go over the boughs again: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow. When thou gatherest the grapes of thy vineyard, thou shalt not glean it afterward: it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow.

> Exodus 23:10–11 — And six years thou shalt sow thy land, and shalt gather in the fruits thereof: But the seventh year thou shalt let it rest and lie still; that the poor of thy people may eat …

Three features of the gleaning laws deserve attention.

**They are a legal charge on the owner, not an act of generosity.** The farmer is *commanded* to under-harvest. The corners of the field are not his to give; they have been assigned away from him by statute.

**They are not means-tested.** There is no assessment, no application, no inquiry into why the gleaner is poor. Anyone who is poor, and any resident foreigner, may come. The law defines a class and opens the field.

**They require the recipient to work.** Gleaning is labour — bending in the sun behind the reapers for a day's grain. The book of Ruth is the system in operation:

> Ruth 2:2 — And Ruth the Moabitess said unto Naomi, Let me now go to the field, and glean ears of corn after him in whose sight I shall find grace. And she said unto her, Go, my daughter.

> Ruth 2:7 — … so she came, and hath continued even from the morning until now, that she tarried a little in the house.

Ruth is a destitute foreign widow. She is not given a basket; she is given *access to a field*, and she works from morning until evening in it. And then Boaz does something the law did not require:

> Ruth 2:15–16 — And when she was risen up to glean, Boaz commanded his young men, saying, Let her glean even among the sheaves, and reproach her not: And let fall also some of the handfuls of purpose for her, and leave them, that she may glean them, and rebuke her not.

He has the reapers drop extra grain where she will find it — making her work more productive without taking it away from her, and specifically forbidding his men to shame her. It is a small masterpiece of assistance: dignity preserved, effort required, outcome quietly guaranteed.

This is the closest thing in Scripture to a designed welfare system, and its shape is neither of the two positions people usually argue about. It is not a handout, and it is not a refusal. It is an opportunity to work, provided at cost to the well-off, with the shortfall silently covered.

The Law is also emphatic that the shape of the system does not license hard-heartedness in operating it:

> Deuteronomy 15:7–8 — If there be among you a poor man of one of thy brethren within any of thy gates in thy land which the LORD thy God giveth thee, thou shalt not harden thine heart, nor shut thine hand from thy poor brother: But thou shalt open thine hand wide unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need, in that which he wanteth.

> Deuteronomy 15:9 — Beware that there be not a thought in thy wicked heart, saying, The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand; and thine eye be evil against thy poor brother, and thou givest him nought; and he cry unto the LORD against thee, and it be sin unto thee.

> Deuteronomy 15:10–11 — Thou shalt surely give him, and thine heart shall not be grieved when thou givest unto him: because that for this thing the LORD thy God shall bless thee in all thy works … For the poor shall never cease out of the land: therefore I command thee, saying, Thou shalt open thine hand wide unto thy brother, to thy poor, and to thy needy, in thy land.

Deuteronomy 15:9 is remarkable: it anticipates a *calculated* reason for withholding — the loan will be cancelled in the coming sabbatical year, so lending is a bad investment — and calls the calculation a wicked thought. The reasoning is sound and the Law forbids it anyway. It is the clearest signal in the Old Testament that good reasons for not giving are exactly the ones to be suspicious of in yourself.

> Leviticus 25:35 — And if thy brother be waxen poor, and fallen in decay with thee; then thou shalt relieve him: yea, though he be a stranger, or a sojourner; that he may live with thee.

And the corresponding severity toward those who profit from the labour of the poor:

> Deuteronomy 24:14–15 — Thou shalt not oppress an hired servant that is poor and needy … At his day thou shalt give him his hire, neither shall the sun go down upon it; for he is poor, and setteth his heart upon it: lest he cry against thee unto the LORD, and it be sin unto thee.

> Jeremiah 22:13 — Woe unto him that buildeth his house by unrighteousness, and his chambers by wrong; that useth his neighbour's service without wages, and giveth him not for his work;

> James 5:4 — Behold, the hire of the labourers who have reaped down your fields, which is of you kept back by fraud, crieth: and the cries of them which have reaped are entered into the ears of the Lord of sabaoth.

Scripture is as hard on the employer who will not pay as on the man who will not work. Both appear before the same court.

## 13. "Because no man hath hired us"

Jesus tells a parable about idle men, and it is not the parable anyone expects.

> Matthew 20:3 — And he went out about the third hour, and saw others standing idle in the marketplace,

> Matthew 20:6–7 — And about the eleventh hour he went out, and found others standing idle, and saith unto them, Why stand ye here all the day idle? They say unto him, Because no man hath hired us. He saith unto them, Go ye also into the vineyard; and whatsoever is right, that shall ye receive.

> Matthew 20:9–10 — And when they came that were hired about the eleventh hour, they received every man a penny. But when the first came, they supposed that they should have received more; and they likewise received every man a penny.

The word translated "idle" (ἀργός) is the ordinary Greek for unemployed or unoccupied. These men have stood in the market all day — the ancient equivalent of the day-labour corner — and nobody hired them. The householder asks the accusing question, *"Why stand ye here all the day idle?"*, and receives the answer that settles it: *because no man hath hired us.* He does not argue. He hires them for the last hour and pays them a full day's wage.

Set the parable next to 2 Thessalonians 3:10 and you have the two cases laid side by side by Scripture itself. Paul's man will not work. Matthew's men cannot get work. The same English word — *idle* — covers both, and treating them alike is precisely the error the two texts together exist to prevent.

The parable also happens to end with a rebuke aimed at the people keeping score:

> Matthew 20:15 — Is it not lawful for me to do what I will with mine own? Is thine eye evil, because I am good?

## 14. What Jesus says about giving

Jesus's own instructions on giving contain no worthiness test anywhere, and in one passage he rules the question out explicitly.

> Matthew 5:42 — Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away.

> Luke 6:30 — Give to every man that asketh of thee; and of him that taketh away thy goods ask them not again.

> Luke 6:34–35 — And if ye lend to them of whom ye hope to receive, what thank have ye? for sinners also lend to sinners, to receive as much again. But love ye your enemies, and do good, and lend, hoping for nothing again; and your reward shall be great, and ye shall be the children of the Highest: for he is kind unto the unthankful and to the evil.

That last clause is the hinge. Jesus grounds the command to give without expectation of return in God's own practice of being kind *to people who do not deserve it* — "the unthankful and the evil." The standard he sets is explicitly not the standard of desert.

> Luke 6:36 — Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.

> Luke 14:12–14 — … When thou makest a dinner or a supper, call not thy friends, nor thy brethren, neither thy kinsmen, nor thy rich neighbours; lest they also bid thee again, and a recompence be made thee. But when thou makest a feast, call the poor, the maimed, the lame, the blind: And thou shalt be blessed; for they cannot recompense thee: for thou shalt be recompensed at the resurrection of the just.

And the Good Samaritan, whose whole point is that the man in the ditch is not asked who he is, how he got there, or whether he had it coming:

> Luke 10:33–35 — But a certain Samaritan, as he journeyed, came where he was: and when he saw him, he had compassion on him, And went to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring in oil and wine, and set him on his own beast, and brought him to an inn, and took care of him. And on the morrow when he departed, he took out two pence, and gave them to the host, and said unto him, Take care of him; and whatsoever thou spendest more, when I come again, I will repay thee.

The lawyer had asked "who is my neighbour?" — a question about the boundaries of obligation, the same species of question as "does this man deserve help?" Jesus answers by telling a story in which nobody asks it, and then reverses the question entirely: not *who qualifies as my neighbour*, but *which of these was neighbour to him?*

## 15. The judgment scene with no means test

> Matthew 25:35–36 — For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me in: Naked, and ye clothed me: I was sick, and ye visited me: I was in prison, and ye came unto me.

> Matthew 25:40 — And the King shall answer and say unto them, Verily I say unto you, Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me.

> Matthew 25:42–43 — For I was an hungred, and ye gave me no meat: I was thirsty, and ye gave me no drink: I was a stranger, and ye took me not in: naked, and ye clothed me not: sick, and in prison, and ye visited me not.

> Matthew 25:45 — … Inasmuch as ye did it not to one of the least of these, ye did it not to me.

Six needs are listed, twice. In none of the twelve clauses is any information given about why the person was hungry, thirsty, naked, ill, foreign, or imprisoned. The prisoner in particular may well be guilty; the text does not care. The sole question at the judgment is whether the need was met.

This is the single most sobering text in the discussion, and it is a warning to a particular temperament. The people on the left hand in that scene are not described as cruel. They are described as having *not done it* — and the parable gives them no chance to explain their reasons. Whatever good reasons they had, and people always have reasons, the King does not ask for them.

> James 2:15–16 — If a brother or sister be naked, and destitute of daily food, And one of you say unto them, Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled; notwithstanding ye give them not those things which are needful to the body; what doth it profit?

> 1 John 3:17 — But whoso hath this world's good, and seeth his brother have need, and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him, how dwelleth the love of God in him?

> 1 John 3:18 — My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth.

## 16. The apostolic church: relief, and the one place it is rationed

The first Christian communities ran organised relief from the start.

> Acts 2:44–45 — And all that believed were together, and had all things common; And sold their possessions and goods, and parted them to all men, as every man had need.

> Acts 4:34–35 — Neither was there any among them that lacked: for as many as were possessors of lands or houses sold them, and brought the prices of the things that were sold, And laid them down at the apostles' feet: and distribution was made unto every man according as he had need.

> Acts 6:1 — And in those days, when the number of the disciples was multiplied, there arose a murmuring of the Grecians against the Hebrews, because their widows were neglected in the daily ministration.

The criterion in Acts is stated twice, identically: *as every man had need.* Acts 6 records the church's first administrative crisis, and it is a complaint about people being *under*-served; the apostles respond by appointing seven men to run the distribution properly, not by tightening it.

> Acts 11:29 — Then the disciples, every man according to his ability, determined to send relief unto the brethren which dwelt in Judaea:

> 2 Corinthians 8:13–14 — For I mean not that other men be eased, and ye burdened: But by an equality, that now at this time your abundance may be a supply for their want, that their abundance also may be a supply for your want: that there may be equality:

> 2 Corinthians 9:7 — Every man according as he purposeth in his heart, so let him give; not grudgingly, or of necessity: for God loveth a cheerful giver.

And then, in the Pastoral Epistles, the one place in the New Testament where relief is explicitly rationed — the widows' roll:

> 1 Timothy 5:3 — Honour widows that are widows indeed.

> 1 Timothy 5:4 — But if any widow have children or nephews, let them learn first to shew piety at home, and to requite their parents: for that is good and acceptable before God.

> 1 Timothy 5:8 — But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel.

> 1 Timothy 5:9–10 — Let not a widow be taken into the number under threescore years old, having been the wife of one man, Well reported of for good works; if she have brought up children, if she have lodged strangers, if she have washed the saints' feet, if she have relieved the afflicted, if she have diligently followed every good work.

> 1 Timothy 5:13 — And withal they learn to be idle, wandering about from house to house; and not only idle, but tattlers also and busybodies, speaking things which they ought not.

> 1 Timothy 5:16 — If any man or woman that believeth have widows, let them relieve them, and let not the church be charged; that it may relieve them that are widows indeed.

This passage is the best evidence that the New Testament does contemplate limits on church support, and it deserves to be read honestly by both sides.

**For the hard reading:** here is an apostolic instruction that sets eligibility criteria — an age threshold, a character requirement, a duty on relatives to pay first — and explicitly excludes a category of applicants from the roll. The church's resources are finite and Paul rations them. He also uses the same vocabulary as 2 Thessalonians 3: the younger widows "learn to be idle … and busybodies," which is the very pairing of 2 Thessalonians 3:11.

**Against the hard reading:** every one of the restrictions exists to protect the fund *for the neediest*, and Paul says so in v. 16 — "that it may relieve them that are widows indeed." The rationing is triage, not refusal. The younger widows are not left destitute; Paul expects them to remarry (v. 14), which in that society meant a household and support. Relatives are made to pay first (v. 4), and the sanction for failing is the harshest language in the chapter, aimed not at the recipient but at the family member who dodges: "worse than an infidel" (v. 8). Note where the severity falls. It falls on the person who *will not support a dependant* — not on the dependant.

So 1 Timothy 5 supports the proposition that a church may set rules for its ongoing support, and refutes the proposition that the rules exist to shrink the number of people helped. Paul's rules exist so that the money reaches the woman who has nobody.

## 17. Two burdens

In the space of four verses Galatians says two things that look contradictory, and the apparent contradiction is the whole ethic in miniature.

> Galatians 6:2 — Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.

> Galatians 6:5 — For every man shall bear his own burden.

The Greek uses two different words. In v. 2 it is *baros* — a weight, a crushing load, more than a person can carry. In v. 5 it is *phortion* — a pack, the load a soldier or traveller carries as a matter of course, one's own proper portion.

Read together: *carry your own pack, and help carry what crushes your brother.* Neither cancels the other. A man who expects others to carry his ordinary pack has misread v. 2; a man who watches his brother crushed and quotes v. 5 at him has misread both.

And the paragraph ends where the Thessalonian one ends:

> Galatians 6:9–10 — And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. As we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith.

"Unto all men, **especially** unto them who are of the household of faith" — the church first, but not the church only.

## 18. Where Scripture leaves the burden of proof

Set the two bodies of material against each other and count.

| | Texts | Conditions attached |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Withhold from the idle** | 2 Thessalonians 3:10, within its paragraph; the sluggard proverbs (which describe consequences rather than command withholding); 1 Timothy 5:9–13 (rationing a specific roll) | Unwillingness, known personally, inside a congregation, as discipline aimed at restoration |
| **Give to the needy** | Deuteronomy 15:7–11; Leviticus 19:9–10; 25:35; Deuteronomy 24:19–21; Proverbs 3:27–28; 14:31; 19:17; 21:13; 22:9; 28:27; Psalms 41:1; 112:9; Isaiah 58:6–10; Matthew 5:42; 25:31–46; Luke 6:30–36; 10:30–37; 14:12–14; Acts 2:44–45; 4:34–35; 11:29; 2 Corinthians 8–9; Galatians 6:10; James 2:15–16; 1 John 3:17 | None |

That is the honest tally, and it should govern how the one verse is used. A single narrowly-framed exception does not overturn a rule stated dozens of times without qualification; it carves a defined space inside it.

There is a second asymmetry worth naming, and it runs through the whole canon. Scripture repeatedly warns the *giver* about the danger of hardness — Deuteronomy 15:9's "wicked thought," Proverbs 21:13's stopped ears, Matthew 25's people who simply did not do it, 1 John 3:17's shut-up compassion. It warns the *recipient* about the danger of idleness far less often, and when it does, it addresses him directly (2 Thessalonians 3:12, "that with quietness they work") rather than handing the warning to someone else to apply on his behalf.

The practical rule this suggests is uncomfortable and hard to get around: **Scripture gives you the sluggard texts to examine yourself with, and the mercy texts to treat other people with.** The reverse — using the sluggard texts on others and the mercy texts on yourself — is available to anyone who wants it, and is the standing temptation of this whole subject.

# Part III — What Christians Have Done With It

The verse has a history, and it is a genuinely mixed one. Understanding how it has been used is the best protection against using it badly.

## 19. The Didache

The earliest surviving application is also one of the best. The *Didache*, a church manual probably from around AD 100 and possibly earlier, gives rules for receiving travelling Christians:

> "But receive everyone who comes in the name of the Lord, and prove and know him afterward; for you shall have understanding right and left. If he who comes is a wayfarer, assist him as far as you are able; but he shall not remain with you more than two or three days, if need be. But if he wants to stay with you, and is an artisan, let him work and eat. But if he has no trade, according to your understanding, see to it that, as a Christian, he shall not live with you idle. But if he wills not to do, he is a Christ-monger. Watch that you keep away from such."
> — *Didache* 12 (Roberts–Donaldson translation)

Look at the order of operations, because it is exactly Paul's:

1. **Receive everyone** who comes. Hospitality first, no screening at the door.
2. **Help the traveller** as far as you are able, no questions.
3. **If he settles and has a trade**, he works and eats.
4. **If he has no trade**, the *community* takes responsibility for finding him something — "according to your understanding, see to it" — so that he is not idle. The obligation to solve the problem falls on the church, not on him alone.
5. **Only then**, if he refuses the arrangement the church has made, is he named a *christemporos* — a Christ-monger, a trafficker in Christ, someone using the name of Jesus as a livelihood.

Refusal is the fifth step and the last. Four steps of open-handed help come first, including a positive duty on the congregation to find work for a man who has none. A church following the *Didache* would have been hard to sponge off and almost impossible to go hungry in.

## 20. Augustine and the monks who would not work

Around AD 400, Aurelius the bishop of Carthage had a problem: monks in his diocese who had given up work on principle, were begging in public, and were citing Scripture in their defence. He asked Augustine to deal with it, and Augustine wrote *De opere monachorum* — *On the Work of Monks*, the only treatise from the early church devoted entirely to this subject.

Their argument was a good one, on its face. Jesus said:

> Matthew 6:26 — Behold the fowls of the air: for they sow not, neither do they reap, nor gather into barns; yet your heavenly Father feedeth them. Are ye not much better than they?

> Matthew 6:34 — Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.

They took no thought for the morrow; they gave themselves to prayer, psalms and hymns; and they held that this spiritual labour satisfied anything Paul could have meant by work. Augustine's reply, across the treatise, was that they had read selectively — that the same apostle who told them to pray without ceasing worked with his hands night and day, that "spiritual work" is not what ἐργάζεσθαι means in 2 Thessalonians 3, and that Matthew 6 forbids anxiety, not employment.

Two things about this episode are worth keeping.

**First, the original patristic use of the verse was against the pious, not against the poor.** Augustine aimed it at men who had made a religion of not working and were being fed by other people's labour — which is very close to Paul's own target.

**Second, it shows how the verse gets its edge.** Augustine could use it because the monks plainly *could* work, plainly *chose* not to, and were plainly living off the community. All three conditions were satisfied and visible. That is the situation the verse was written for.

## 21. Chrysostom on both sides of the question

John Chrysostom preached through 2 Thessalonians in Antioch in the 390s, and his handling of chapter 3 is the most instructive thing in the tradition, because the same preacher who expounded this verse also preached the fiercest sermons in Christian antiquity against inspecting the poor.

On the verse itself, he read v. 10 and v. 13 as one thought:

> "For as he had said, 'if any does not work neither let him eat,' fearing lest they should perish by hunger, he has added, 'But in doing good, be not ye weary.'"
> — *Homily V on 2 Thessalonians*

And in the homilies on Lazarus and the rich man, preaching to a congregation full of people who wanted to know whether beggars deserved their alms, he closed the question off:

> "If you wish to show kindness, you must not require an accounting of a person's life, but merely correct his poverty and fill his need."

> "The poor man has one plea, his want and his standing in need: do not require anything else from him; but even if he is the most wicked of all men and is at a loss for his necessary sustenance, let us free him from hunger."

> "When you see on earth the man who has encountered the shipwreck of poverty, do not judge him, do not seek an account of his life, but free him from his misfortune."

> "Charity is so called because we give it even to the unworthy."
> — John Chrysostom, *On Wealth and Poverty* (homilies on Lazarus and the rich man)

These are the same man, on the same Scriptures, within a few years. He is not confused, and he is not contradicting himself. He is doing what Paul did: applying the rule inside the congregation, where the church knows the man and is responsible for him, and refusing to let it out into the street, where it becomes an excuse for the comfortable to interrogate the desperate.

Chrysostom's position, if it had to be put in one sentence: *the church may tell its own idle members to go to work; it may not stand at the door with a questionnaire.*

## 22. The monastic rules

The monastic tradition made work a spiritual discipline, and 2 Thessalonians 3:10 is one of its foundation texts. Basil of Caesarea, in the *Longer Rules* (fourth century), made the verse central to his insistence that piety is no excuse for idleness — and tied the monks' labour directly to the relief of the poor: the community works so that there is something to give away. Benedict's *Rule* (sixth century) opens its chapter on manual labour with the line that became a proverb:

> "Idleness is the enemy of the soul. Therefore the brothers should have specified periods for manual labour as well as for prayerful reading."
> — *Rule of St Benedict* 48

The monastic reading is the healthiest in the tradition, because it turns the verse on the person quoting it. In Basil and Benedict, 2 Thessalonians 3:10 is not a rule for deciding who to help. It is a rule for how *you* are to live — and the reason given for your labour is that it produces something for somebody else.

---

## 23. The Reformers

Calvin's commentary on 2 Thessalonians 3:10 makes two moves that have held up.

He is unembarrassed about the principle: "indolence and idleness are accursed of God," and man was created to do something — a thing Scripture teaches and nature itself taught the pagans.

But he defines the command's target precisely. The instruction, he says, was not given to the idle men at all; it was given to the church:

> "He does not mean that he gave commandment to those persons, but forbade that the Thessalonians should encourage their indolence by supplying them with food."

And he widens what counts as work, against a narrow reading: there are different kinds of labour, and whoever benefits society by his industry — ruling a household, administering affairs, giving counsel, teaching — is working.

Luther's practical contribution was of a different kind. He attacked the medieval economy of mendicancy, arguing that organised begging was bad for both the beggar and the giver, and pushed for parish-level provision instead: in the Leisnig ordinance of 1523 and the "common chest" schemes that followed it, each town was to fund its own poor from a central fund, with the able-bodied found work and the genuinely helpless supported. The verse was part of that argument — but so was the assumption that the community owed its poor something better than a coin at the church door.

---

## 24. The Poor Laws and the "undeserving poor"

The verse's darkest career begins here. From the Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601 onwards, English relief was built around a distinction between the *impotent poor* — the old, the sick, the disabled, the orphaned, who were to be relieved — and the *sturdy beggar* or *vagabond*, the able-bodied idle, who was to be set to work, and punished if he refused. 2 Thessalonians 3:10 was the text of choice for the second half of that framework.

In principle the distinction is Paul's own, and defensible. In practice, across three centuries, it went wrong in a predictable way: the *burden of proof* migrated. What began as "we will not subsidise a man who refuses work" became, by the time of the New Poor Law of 1834 and the workhouse system, something closer to "an applicant is presumed idle until he proves otherwise, and relief must be made unpleasant enough to deter him." The doctrine of *less eligibility* — that relief must leave the recipient worse off than the lowest-paid labourer — made deterrence the design goal.

Two failures compound in that history.

**The empirical failure.** The framework assumed that work was available to anyone who genuinely wanted it, so that continued poverty was evidence of a defective will. In an industrial economy with trade cycles, that assumption was simply false, and a large proportion of those judged undeserving were men in the position of the labourers in Matthew 20:7 — standing in the market because nobody had hired them.

**The moral failure.** The Pauline procedure was inverted. Paul's rule runs from knowledge of a particular brother to a judgment about him. The Poor Law ran from a judgment about a class to a policy applied to individuals nobody knew. Paul's rule ends in restoration; the workhouse ended in deterrence. And the guard clause — "be not weary in well doing" — dropped out entirely.

This is the history that makes people flinch when the verse is quoted, and the flinch is not unreasonable. It is also not a reason to give the verse up. It is a reason to insist on the conditions the verse itself sets.

---

## 25. Jamestown

In the spring of 1609, with the Virginia colony starving and a third of the settlers refusing to work, Captain John Smith assembled them and put the verse into English law for the first time in America:

> "You see now that power resteth wholly in myself: you must obey this now for a Law, that he that will not work shall not eat (except by sickness he be disabled). For the labors of thirty or forty honest and industrious men shall not be consumed to maintain an hundred and fifty idle loiterers."
> — John Smith, as reported in *The Generall Historie of Virginia* (1624)

The line is remembered for its severity. What deserves to be remembered is the parenthesis: **"except by sickness he be disabled."** Even in a starving colony, under martial law, from a soldier not otherwise noted for tenderness, the exception is stated in the same breath as the rule. Smith understood what the verse said. The people who quote his first eight words and drop his next six do not.

His stated reason is also worth noting, because it is Paul's: not that the idle deserve to suffer, but that the labour of the industrious few was being consumed to maintain the idle many. The rule protects the workers, not the food supply.

---

## 26. Lenin

The most famous modern use of the verse is not Christian at all.

Lenin took up "He who does not work, neither shall he eat" in 1918, during the civil war and the grain crisis, as a slogan of the new state. It was written into the RSFSR Constitution of 1918, whose Article 18 declared labour the duty of every citizen, and into Article 12 of the Stalin Constitution of 1936 in the familiar biblical form.

In Soviet usage the sentence was aimed at a class rather than at an individual: not at the man who will not work but at those who lived on others' labour — rentiers, the former owning classes, and later anyone the state chose to designate a "parasite," a category eventually given its own criminal statute.

The point of including this is not to score against anyone. It is that the verse has been used with equal enthusiasm from opposite ends of the political spectrum, by people arguing for less public provision and by people arguing for total state control of labour. A text that can be conscripted that easily is a text that is being used as a slogan rather than read. In both cases, the same three things go missing: the word *unwilling*, the word *brother*, and the sentence "be not weary in well doing."

---

## 27. What the history shows

Laid end to end, the record has a shape.

**When the verse has been used well** — the *Didache*, Augustine on the monks, Basil and Benedict, Calvin's reading — four features recur:

- it is applied *inside* a community, to people who are known;
- it is applied to those who plainly *could* work;
- the community takes responsibility for making work available before it withholds anything;
- and the person quoting it is applying it to himself or to his own institution.

**When it has gone wrong** — the workhouse, and the slogan uses — the same four features are inverted:

- it is applied to strangers, in bulk;
- to people whose ability to work is assumed rather than known;
- with no obligation on anyone to make work available;
- and by people applying it to somebody else.

That is a usable test, and the last item is the most reliable of the four. The verse behaves well in the mouth of someone who is including himself in its demand, and badly in the mouth of someone who is not.

# Part IV — Using the Verse Without Misusing It

## 28. Seven questions to ask first

If you are weighing whether 2 Thessalonians 3:10 applies to a decision in front of you, these are the questions the passage itself raises, in the order it raises them.

**1. Is this person unwilling, or unable?**
The entire verse turns on this and nothing else. Illness, disability, mental illness, addiction, injury, age, caring responsibilities, a criminal record, no transport, no address, no papers, a collapsed local economy — in every one of these the will may be perfectly intact and the capacity absent. If you do not know which you are looking at, you do not yet know whether the verse applies.

**2. Do I actually know this person?**
Paul's rule is addressed to a congregation about a member they see every week. Applied to someone you have known for ninety seconds, it is not the same rule. If you cannot answer question 1 from knowledge, the honest position is that the verse gives you no guidance here, and the general commands about the poor — which need no such knowledge — are the ones in force.

**3. Is there work for him to do, and have I offered any?**
The *Didache* makes this the community's job: if he has no trade, *see to it* that he is not idle. Paul's rule presupposes an economy where a willing man could get work. Withholding on the grounds that a man will not work, when no work has been offered and none is available, is punishing him for the state of the labour market.

**4. Is this the church's ongoing support, or is this an emergency?**
Paul is regulating a standing arrangement — the common table, the regular dole. Nothing in the passage addresses acute need: a person who has not eaten today, is about to be evicted, or is in danger. Those are Matthew 25 and Luke 10 situations, and no worthiness test is applied in either. A church may reasonably decline to fund a member's chosen idleness indefinitely and still feed him tonight.

**5. Have I done step two?**
Verse 12 comes before verse 10 is enforced: tell him, plainly and kindly, to go to work. If the conversation has never happened, the sanction is premature.

**6. Am I aiming at his restoration, or at being rid of him?**
"That he may be ashamed" and "count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" are not optional trimmings. If you would be relieved never to see him again, you are not doing what this passage commands, whatever else you are doing.

**7. Whose bread am I actually withholding?**
Paul stops a *subsidy*. He does not organise a boycott, block anyone else's charity, or seek to make the man's life harder in any other respect. The sanction is "we will not fund this," full stop.

If questions 1–3 come out on the side of "I don't know," "no," and "no," then the verse does not reach your situation — and the texts that do reach it are the ones in Part II.

---

## 29. What the verse authorises

Stated positively, so it is not lost in the qualifications. On its own terms, 2 Thessalonians 3:10 genuinely establishes all of this:

- **A church may decline to subsidise a member's chosen idleness.** This is not uncharitable. It is apostolic instruction, given twice, in person and in writing.
- **There is such a thing as a wrong claim on other people's labour.** Paul does not treat all need as equivalent, and neither should anyone else. The man in verse 11 was wronging the people who fed him.
- **Ordinary work is a Christian duty**, not a regrettable necessity, and the Christian norm is to eat one's own bread (v. 12) and to have something over to give away (Ephesians 4:28).
- **Refusing to support idleness is a form of love, not a suspension of it.** The goal in v. 14 is the man's shame, which is to say his repentance, which is to say his good. Indefinitely funding a man's refusal to work is not kindness to him; Paul evidently thought it was harm.
- **A church may set rules for its ongoing relief.** 1 Timothy 5 does exactly that — precisely so the fund reaches those who have nobody.
- **The person who will not provide for his own dependants is under the severest censure in the chapter** (1 Timothy 5:8). Duty to work is first of all a duty to the people who rely on you.

---

## 30. What it does not authorise

- **Refusing help to anyone whose willingness you have not established.** The condition is unwillingness; no unwillingness, no case.
- **Treating poverty as presumptive evidence of idleness.** This is the historical failure of section 24, and Matthew 20:7 is the answer to it.
- **Withholding emergency relief.** Nothing in the passage is about acute need, and Matthew 25 is.
- **Using it on strangers.** The passage is about a brother, inside the congregation, under discipline.
- **Contempt, scorn, or writing anyone off.** Verse 15 forbids it explicitly.
- **Settling a political argument.** The verse is real evidence about work and dependency, and people may honestly reach different policy conclusions from it. What no one can do is claim it *settles* the question, because it was written to a congregation about its own members and says nothing about the state.
- **Cooling your general generosity.** Verse 13 exists to prevent exactly this, and it was put there by the man who wrote verse 10.

---

## 31. Hard cases

The verse was given for hard cases; here are the ones that come up.

**A man in your church has quit a perfectly good job, refuses to look for another, and is living on the benevolence fund.** This is Paul's case almost exactly. Follow the procedure: talk to him (v. 12); if nothing changes, stop the fund (v. 10); keep talking to him (v. 15); take him back the moment he moves. Do not skip to the end.

**Someone asks you for money in the street.** The verse does not apply — you do not know him, cannot assess willingness, and are not his church. You are in Luke 6:30 and Matthew 25. If you are worried about what the money will be spent on, buy food instead; the concern is legitimate and there are answers to it that do not require you to decide whether a stranger deserves to eat.

**Addiction.** Willingness is precisely what addiction damages, which makes this the hardest case there is. "Will not" and "cannot" are genuinely entangled, and the honest answer is that no one can reliably tell them apart from outside. Two things can be held together: it can be right to refuse cash that will go straight into the addiction, *and* wrong to refuse food, shelter, medical help, or your continued presence in the person's life. Refusing a specific form of help is not the same as withdrawing help. And verse 15 — *admonish him as a brother* — is the part of the passage most likely to keep you in the room, which is where you need to be.

**Mental illness that presents as idleness.** Severe depression looks exactly like not wanting to work, from the outside, and is not. If there is any question of it, question 1 is unanswered and the verse is not in play. Get him to a doctor before you get him to a job.

**Someone who has been out of work for years and has stopped trying.** Discouragement is not the same as refusal, though it can harden into something like it. This is a case for work, not for withholding: the *Didache*'s instruction to the community — *see to it* that he is not idle — is far more useful here than Paul's sanction. Find him something to do before deciding he will not do anything.

**A family member who will not work.** Here the verse and 1 Timothy 5:4, 8 have to be held together: you have a duty to provide for your own household, *and* funding an adult's refusal to work may be the thing preventing him from working. There is no formula. What the passage rules out is either extreme — abandoning him, or subsidising him indefinitely while saying nothing (v. 12).

**A church deciding a benevolence policy.** Write down the 1 Timothy 5 logic: rules exist so the money reaches those with nobody. Fund emergencies without inquiry. For ongoing support, set terms — and make finding work part of the church's job, not only the recipient's. Review it annually, and notice which way the drift runs: policies in this area drift toward tightness, never toward generosity, and verse 13 is aimed at the drift.

---

## 32. If you are the one being refused

Not everyone reading this is on the deciding end.

If someone has quoted this verse at you, the questions to ask yourself are the ones Paul asks — is the problem that you will not, or that you cannot? — and they are worth asking honestly, because the verse was written to be applied by the man himself first. Paul addresses the idlers directly in v. 12 before he says anything about them to anyone else.

But three things should be said just as plainly.

If you are unable — sick, disabled, caring for someone, out of work and looking — then **the verse is not about you**, and whoever quoted it at you has misread it. You are entitled to say so, and the Greek is on your side.

If you have been refused help with contempt, that was a violation of verse 15 by the person who refused you, whatever the merits of the refusal itself.

And if the answer is that you will not — that is a thing that can change, and Paul's whole procedure exists to make the change possible. The sanction in verse 14 has a purpose stated in the same sentence, and the purpose is not that you should go away.

---

## 33. The whole thing on one page

**The verse exists.** 2 Thessalonians 3:10: "if any would not work, neither should he eat." It is apostolic, it is binding, and it means what it says.

**"Would not" means "is not willing to."** The Greek *ou thelei* is about the will. The unemployed, the sick, the disabled, the caring, the old, and the unhireable are not in view and never were.

**It is a rule for a church about its own members.** Every verse around it says "brother." It regulates a congregation's support of a man it knows, whom it has already told to go to work, and whom it intends to restore.

**Paul guarded it himself.** "But ye, brethren, be not weary in well doing" is three verses later, and "count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" is five.

**The rest of Scripture is not neutral.** Dozens of commands to give to the poor carry no worthiness test at all. Israel's own welfare law required work of the recipient and provided the field for it at the owner's expense. Jesus grounds giving in God's kindness "unto the unthankful and to the evil." At the last judgment nobody is asked why the hungry were hungry.

**The history is a warning.** Used inside a community by people including themselves in its demand, the verse has produced the *Didache* and the Rule of Benedict. Used on strangers in bulk by people exempting themselves, it has produced the workhouse.

**So: yes, there is such a verse — and it says less than it is usually made to say, and asks more of the person quoting it than of the person it is quoted against.**
